One Yoga Studio, End to End

One small owner-operated yoga studio, end to end: the perishable seat, the price ladder, the paid intro, and the two leaks a consultant can sell — lead response at the top of the funnel and payment chasedown at the bottom. Executive summary — the one-pager

Read this in a couple of minutes. It is concepts only. Every figure lives in the detail sections below.

  • A yoga studio sells a habit wrapped around a seat that expires. The room has a fixed number of seats and the class happens whether they are full or not. Profit lives in which seats are empty, at what time of day — not in the blended average.
  • Memberships, packs, and drop-ins are three different customers. The member prepays for a habit, the pack buyer prepays for flexibility with an expiry date, the drop-in pays the highest price for zero commitment. A good price ladder makes membership the obvious bargain; a bad one lets the best customers stay on packs forever.
  • The front door should be paid, not free. A paid intro selects for intent and starts a habit; a free class attracts people who were never going to buy. What converts a trial buyer is not the offer but attendance in the first two weeks.
  • Leak one: leads die in silence. Prospects message several studios in one evening and buy from the one that replies first with a warm answer and a named next step. The fix is an automatic acknowledgement in minutes, a human reply within one business hour, and a five-touch sequence through the trial. The effect compounds, because every converted member stays for months.
  • Leak two: sold is not collected. In Mindbody, a failed autopay converted to a negative balance shows as "Successful" on the autopay report, and failed direct-debit payments never appear there at all. Arrears only surface in the Account Balances report, and members can keep attending while owing money. Most of the money that ever comes back comes back in the first ten days.
  • Members follow teachers, not rooms. The teacher is the retention strategy, the pay model decides the studio's economics, and teacher training is a lumpy profit engine that feeds its own competition. ClassPass is a filler for empty off-peak seats, not a funnel.

Figures are from the research report (Nov 7, 2026), with provenance and caveats below. They are US, 2024–2026 figures and vary by locality — use them as a diagnostic model, not as a quote to a client.

1. The room: perishable seats, measured per class

A single-room studio runs about 25–30 classes a week in 1,200–2,500 sq ft, with 10–15 students as the boutique optimum for attention and retention and 15–30 as the standard-studio cap. Occupancy is attended seats divided by available seats, per class and per daypart, never as a single blended weekly average. A 95% peak and a 30% Tuesday afternoon average to a respectable-looking 62%, and the blended number hides the problem. Bookings are not attendance: a class can be 95% booked and 76% utilised after no-shows.

The benchmarks converge across four vendor sources and were verified live: 70% attended utilisation is the floor, 75–82% is strong, under 60% signals a closure trajectory inside 12 months, and above 90% sustained is churn and burnout risk. Prime time is different: 85%+ fill is healthy, 92%+ means add capacity, under 75% in prime time means the brand is in trouble even if the blended number looks acceptable. Midday runs far lower, roughly half the seats full. Break-even typically sits around 75–80% occupancy.

No-shows run 15–20% of booked seats in yoga, within a 10–30% boutique range, and 25–30% without active management. The near-universal policy is a 12-hour cancellation window: inside it, pack and drop-in credits are forfeited and unlimited members are charged a fee, typically $10–$15 for a late cancel and $20–$25 for a no-show. Some studios escalate at the third late cancel. The fix is not a stricter policy but an automated waitlist that fills the spot the moment someone cancels, plus reminders before the class. One vendor claim puts automated waitlists at filling about 81% of cancelled spots versus about 34% manual; that was not re-verified, so treat it as directional.

Model example (labelled arithmetic from the report): a 25-class week at 20 seats is 500 seats. At 70% attended that is 350 attended seats a week. At about $16 realised per seat, that is roughly $5,600 a week, about $24,000 a month of class revenue. Instructor pay at $50 a class across 25 classes is about $1,250 a week, roughly 22% of that class revenue.

2. Three customers, one price ladder

A mature studio's revenue mix is roughly 60–75% memberships, 15–20% drop-ins, and 15–30% supplementary (workshops, training, retail, online). The price ladder, verified live against LaunchAdvisor's April 2026 guide and live studio price pages: unlimited $99–$149 a month at mid-market, with 2026 sources pushing the top end to $100–$180 and premium metros to $249; 3×/week $79–$99; drop-in $22–$32; 10-pack $180–$250; 20-pack $320–$450; annual unlimited $900–$1,400, roughly 9–10 months for the price of 12.

The ladder's design rule: price the drop-in high enough that 6+ classes a month makes membership the obvious bargain, and price packs so the per-class cost is clearly inferior to unlimited. Unlimited is roughly 8 times the drop-in price, so a member attending 8 or more classes a month is cheaper on unlimited. Pack expiry discipline matters: 3 months for a 10-pack, 6 months for a 20-pack. Without it, dead pack holders sit on the active roll indefinitely and flatter the member count.

Revenue per member per month is the weakest figure in the prior research and is now flagged as a conflict. LaunchAdvisor is the only source stating $85–$115, apparently counting membership dues only. PulseRevOps, opened live, puts mid-market yoga and barre at $120–$160 all-in and premium at $180–$240. The planning number for a membership-led studio is about $130 a month all-in. Do not quote $85–$115 as "the" yoga revenue per member.

Founding offers are a permanent haircut, not a launch tactic. The standard pattern is 10–20% below the standard rate, locked in permanently, sometimes with a no-break clause: a card decline unresolved within 5 days, or any pause or cancel, permanently forfeits founding status. A studio that sells 100 founding memberships at 20% off a $149 rate gives up about $30 a month per member, about $3,000 a month, for as long as those members stay, in exchange for pre-opening cash. Cap the count, tier the discount, and put a time limit or a no-break clause in writing.

4. Leak one: lead response, the cheapest fix in the building

No published benchmark exists for the share of yoga-studio leads by channel. The consistent qualitative ranking: Google Search and Google Business Profile first for high-intent ("yoga near me"), Instagram and Facebook for discovery, referrals as the highest-quality source (referred clients convert at roughly double the rate of cold traffic and stay months longer), walk-ins and local partnerships, and ClassPass as discovery only. The multi-touch reality matters: a typical journey is a friend mention, an Instagram scroll, a Google search for the name, reviews, then a booking. Asked "how did you hear about us?" they say "Google" because it was last. Judge channels by second-purchase conversion, not first-touch counts.

Speed-to-lead evidence is old and cross-industry, and the report grades it honestly. HBR 2011 (1.25M leads, 2,241 firms): contact within 1 hour is about 7 times more likely to qualify. MIT research: 5 minutes versus 30 minutes is about 21 times more likely to qualify. No yoga-specific speed-to-lead study exists. The defensible consultant line: prospects message 3–5 studios in one evening; the first warm reply with a named next step usually wins. About 30–40% of fitness leads arrive after hours, when the front desk is teaching, not watching the inbox. The operational fix is an automated acknowledgement in minutes (SMS or WhatsApp) plus a human reply within 1 business hour, with after-hours coverage.

The follow-up cadence that works for studios (Mujgos, verified live) is five touches: a warm text before the first class (what to bring, where to park, "we'll meet you at the door"); a "how did it feel?" note after the first class, the same day, from a human; a nudge if they have not rebooked in 3 days; a personal check-in from an instructor mid-trial, not the front desk, from the teacher whose class they took; and the membership offer 2 days before the pass expires. Mujgos: a studio that adds this sequence typically lifts trial-to-member conversion by 10 to 20 points on the exact same traffic. The conversion window is day 12–14 for a two-week trial, and 10–15% of non-converters return within 60 days on a light-touch nurture sequence.

Cost to acquire a member is conflicting and definition-dependent: $45–$120 (IHRSA via vendor, secondhand), $70–$160 for a cold start falling to $40–$80 from month 6 as word of mouth compounds (Vibefam, yoga-specific), $160–$220 in major metros (PulseRevOps 2027), and channel-blended figures up to $239–$557. Studios counting only ad spend understate CAC by 40–60%. The health rule is lifetime value to CAC of at least 3:1 on contribution, not gross. Use the client's own fully-loaded CAC, not a benchmark.

5. Leak two: collections, and what the software hides

Memberships are paid by monthly autopay on a card on file; annual prepay is cash up front. Class packs are paid up front and credits expire. Drop-ins are paid at booking or at the door, and card-on-file at first booking is what makes late-cancel and no-show fees collectible at all. Without a card on file, a $25 no-show fee is a polite request. Workshops, training, and retreats take deposits months ahead, with the balance on instalments.

There is no clean published benchmark for the yoga-studio failed-payment rate. Vendor claims span 3–8% of monthly charges failing on first attempt (Mujgos, verified live; Member Solutions across thousands of schools) to 7–15% (gym-industry upper range). The 3–8% range is the most usable planning figure for a small studio. Causes: expired or replaced cards 45–55%, insufficient funds 25–30%, soft declines 10–15%. The 20–40% "involuntary churn" figure is SaaS data (Recurly, Paddle), not fitness data, and must not be quoted as a yoga benchmark.

Dunning, what good looks like: retry on day 1–3 (soft declines often resolve on first retry), retry day 3–7, retry day 7–14, a card updater (Visa Account Updater / Mastercard Automatic Billing Updater) running in the background, the member notified by email and SMS with a one-tap card-update link, access held at about day 7, suspension or termination at about 2 weeks overdue. The first dunning email within 24 hours has a far higher open rate than a later one. Most of the money that ever comes back comes back in the first 10 days; after 7–14 days unrecovered, the probability of recovery drops below 30% and churn probability exceeds 60% (vendor claim, directional).

Dollar math (labelled arithmetic from the report, 150-member studio): 150 members at $130 average with a 5% first-attempt failure rate is about 8 failed charges a month, about $1,040 a month at risk. At 58% manual recovery, about $437 a month is lost; at 85% automated recovery, about $156 a month is lost — a difference of about $280 a month, about $3,400 a year, before counting the members who churn entirely over a failed payment.

The Mindbody arrears trap, verified in Mindbody's own support docs: with "convert declined credit card autopays to a negative account balance" enabled, after retries the charge is voided, the autopay deleted from the schedule, and the amount becomes a negative balance on the client's house account — and it then shows as "Successful" on the Autopay Detail and Autopay Summary reports. Failed ACH and direct-debit payments never appear on Autopay Detail at all and trigger no failed-payment email. Mindbody's own recommendation is to run the Account Balances report regularly, filtered to negative balances only, aged 0–29 / 30–59 / 60–89 / 90+ days.

6. Teachers: the retention strategy, and the pay model that changes the P&L

Flat per-class pay is the converged range: $25–$75 a class, most classes $30–$50, with per-head bonuses of $1–$4 a student above a threshold, and subs at $35–$55 a class. Instructor pay at 25–35% of class revenue is true for flat-pay group classes at typical fill, and total people costs should stay at or below 40% of gross. But revenue-share deals run 30–60% and higher — one source cites 50–70% — and a studio with revenue-share teachers has a structurally different P&L. The first thing to check is which model the studio is actually on.

There is no published benchmark for yoga-teacher churn or tenure. The most-cited number, "80% of instructors leave every year," is an unsourced Substack claim and must not be used. What is sourced: replacing an instructor costs $3,000–$8,000; members with a strong instructor connection cancel at 18% a year versus 38% without (IHRSA 2024, secondhand); regulars follow a departing teacher within 60–90 days. Contractor versus employee is a legal tripwire, not a pricing choice: per-class pay, control over the schedule, and who sets the rates all feed into classification, and the rules are shifting. This is not legal advice, and the studio should get counsel before reclassifying anyone.

7. The profit engine: teacher training, workshops, retreats

Teacher training at $2,500–$4,500 a student is confirmed by five independent sources, with an upper tail to $5,000 and in-person or destination trainings at $3,000–$8,000 or more. One cohort of 10 students at $3,500 is $35,000, roughly a month and a half of class revenue for a small studio, which is why training is called the profit engine. But it is a year-two-or-later move, not a launch product: it needs a senior lead teacher and a reputation to fill seats. Faculty pay runs about 25% of training revenue per faculty member, or hourly. And it cannibalises its own market: trainings over-produce teachers, and suburbs end up with newly certified teachers willing to teach for $45 a class. Online programs undercut pricing, and advanced 300-hour and specialty modules are where margin remains.

Workshops run $30–$100 a person for a two-hour session, with the instructor typically taking 40–50% of revenue, and attendees convert to membership at 20–40%. Local weekend retreats run $300–$500 a person; international retreats $2,000–$4,000 a person. Retail margins run 40–60%, but retail is a low-volume lobby business for a typical yoga studio, not a revenue line to plan around; the vendor claim of $25–$40 of retail per member per month is boutique-wide and implausibly high for yoga. Privates are the highest-margin under-used line at $85–$150 an hour, with the instructor keeping 40–55%, so the studio keeps roughly $45–$60 on a $100 session versus roughly $25–$35 net on a group class hour at low fill.

8. ClassPass: filler, not funnel

ClassPass pays $7–$14 per reservation in most markets against a $22–$38 drop-in, or roughly 35–50% of the lowest drop-in rate, and converts 3–6% of visitors to direct membership. ClassPass publishes no rate card; its floor is confidential per studio, so $7–$14 is a guide estimate, not a ClassPass-published figure. ClassPass's own position, from its partner FAQ: "ClassPass is NOT a lead generator" and "ClassPass users are unlikely to convert to direct clients." For every 100 direct clients before joining, studios typically have about 102 after. ClassPass pays most for the seats you least need to fill: classes over 80% full with direct members earn a roughly 45% higher ClassPass payout than classes under 50% full.

The caps, from vendor consensus: no more than 15% of revenue or 20% of bookings, never more than 25–30% of any single class, and open ClassPass inventory only for slots below 60% direct fill, off-peak only. Above 25% of revenue it cannibalises direct memberships and takes 90–120 days to unwind. The diagnostic is which class, not whether ClassPass: the same 20 visits in a 90%-full peak class displace direct drop-ins and lose money; in a 40%-full Tuesday noon class they are found money.

9. Churn, lifetime, and win-back

Monthly churn of 3–4% is best-in-class, not typical: the boutique median is 5–7% a month, and the Health & Fitness Association's 2025 annual retention of 66.4% implies about 3.4% a month across all fitness. A studio quoting 3% should be asked whether freezes are counted as active and whether the denominator is members or paying members. Implied lifetime is 1 divided by churn: about 30 months at 3.4%, about 20 months at 5%, about 14 months at 7%. The prior report's "about 14 months" example therefore implies about 7% monthly churn, a median boutique, not a premium one. Frequency is the retention predictor: members hitting 8 or more visits a month show about 90% retention, and the first 90 days account for 40–50% of total annual attrition. Onboarding, not month 11, is where retention is won.

Win-back is the cheapest revenue in the building. Structured win-back recovers 8–15% of cancelled members a year, 12–18% within 6 months with a dedicated sequence versus under 3% baseline without outreach. Timing decays fast: 12–20% in the first 30 days after cancellation, 7–12% in days 31–60, 4–8% in days 61–90, under 4% after 90 days. Catch the attendance drop while the member is still active and drifting, rather than the cancellation: re-engagement probability is about 3 times higher at the drift stage than 30 days post-cancel. A member who re-joins after 90 days away is nearly as churn-prone as a brand-new member and needs a 14-day welcome-back sequence, not just a discount. Labelled arithmetic: a 200-member studio at 5% monthly churn loses 10 members a month, 120 a year. A 12% win-back recovers about 14 a year, each worth about $2,600 gross lifetime value at $130 a month over 20 months, about $37,000 a year of recovered lifetime value from a list the studio already owns.

Correction to the prior reports, stated plainly: the "1-point churn cut is worth $2,200 a month on 200 members at $110" claim was wrong by 10× as a monthly figure. One point of monthly churn on 200 members is 2 members, times $110 is about $220 a month immediately. About $2,200 a month is only the run-rate after roughly 10 months of compounding, as retained members accumulate. The prior report repeated the error from its source, LaunchAdvisor's pricing page, and this dive corrects it.

10. The 10 diagnostic questions — ask verbatim, of any yoga studio owner

Ask for last month's numbers. "I don't know" is itself the diagnosis.

  1. Show me last month: how many inquiries, how many bought an intro offer, how many attended a first class, how many converted to a membership — by source? Green: paid-intro conversion 30–50%; free-class conversion 10–20%. If source is not tracked, the booking system, not marketing, is job one.
  2. What is your monthly churn — members lost ÷ members at the start of the month — counting freezes as not-active — and what is 1 ÷ that number? Green: under 4% a month best-in-class; amber 4–5%; red over 7%.
  3. What did you actually collect per attended seat last month — total class revenue ÷ attended seats — and how does that compare with your $25 drop-in? Green: realised $15–$18 or more per seat on a $22–$32 drop-in ladder. This one ratio exposes ClassPass dilution, founding-rate drag, and pack breakage.
  4. What % of revenue went to instructor pay last month, including subs, and which teachers are on flat vs revenue-share? Green: flat-pay group classes 25–35% of class revenue; total people costs at or below 40% of gross. A revenue-share teacher at 50–60% changes the answer completely.
  5. How many sellable seats did you have last week, and how many were attended — by class and by daypart? Green: blended 75–82%, prime-time 85%+, off-peak honestly reported. A blended 75% can hide a 95% peak and a 40% midday.
  6. What % of revenue is recurring (memberships) vs packs/drop-ins, and what happens to cash if nobody new joins for 60 days? Green: 60–75% membership. Below 40% recurring is fragile. Training and retreat lumpiness must not be counted as recurring.
  7. Show me your Account Balances report filtered to negative-only, aged 90+ days — and what does Autopay Detail show for the same month? Green: negative balances under 1% of monthly dues; every failed charge touched within 10 days. Red: a 90+ day bucket the owner has never opened.
  8. What is your first-response time to a new inquiry — median, not best case — and who owns replies on evenings and weekends? Green: automated acknowledgement in minutes, human reply under 1 business hour. If replies are checked once a day, 30–40% of leads arrived after hours and waited overnight.
  9. Which teacher, if they left tomorrow, would take students with them — and what would that cost you? Green: no single teacher holds over 25% of peak-class attendance; a sub bench exists for every regular slot; replacement cost $3,000–$8,000 budgeted as a risk, not a surprise.
  10. What is your cost to acquire one paying member, fully loaded (ads + intro discount + onboarding time), by channel — and what is that member's lifetime value? Green: lifetime value to CAC of at least 3:1 on contribution, not gross. If CAC is unknown, the marketing budget is a guess. ClassPass members must be costed at $7–$14 realised, not at drop-in.

Lead-response diagnostics

Count, don't ask. Pull last month's inquiries from every channel (website form, Google Business Profile messages, Instagram DMs, phone and voicemail, email, walk-in sheet) and stamp first-response time. Then check the trial pipeline: of last month's intro buyers, how many attended 0 classes (bought and never came, the purest leak), 1 class, 2–4, or 5 or more? The 5+ group is the conversion pool; the 0–1 group is an onboarding failure, not a pricing failure. And was the second class booked before they left the first?

Collections diagnostics

Failed-charge rate: scheduled autopays that failed on first attempt divided by scheduled autopays, last 3 months. There is no benchmark; the trend and the recovery rate are the diagnostics. Recovery rate and speed: what share of failed charges is recovered within 10 days versus written off to negative balance. Arrears ageing: negative balances in the 0–29, 30–59, 60–89, and 90+ day buckets, in dollars and member count. Anyone in 90+ while still attending is a policy failure, not a member failure. Card-on-file coverage: what share of active members has a valid card on file, and what share of cards expires in the next 60 days. Dunning audit: is the card updater on, are retries automatic, does the owner get a business copy of the failed-payment email daily, and is access held at day 7?

Caveats — say these plainly

  • Most benchmarks are vendor guides (LaunchAdvisor, PulseRevOps, Mujgos, Vibefam), not audited data. The only audited figures in this dive are the YogaSix franchise disclosure (average $488,615, median $468,417, 162 studios, FY2024) and the Health & Fitness Association's 66.4% annual retention. Every figure in the report carries a provenance label.
  • Revenue per member per month is disputed: $85–$115 is single-sourced (dues only) and contradicted by $120–$160 all-in. Do not quote one number.
  • Net margin for an independent yoga studio is disputed: about 7% versus 10–20% versus 15–30% versus 35–40% EBITDA. No audited figure exists for independents.
  • Cost to acquire a member is disputed: $45–$120 versus $160–$220 in major metros versus $239–$557 channel-blended. The figures differ on what costs are counted and on market tier.
  • Speed-to-lead multipliers (7× within an hour, 21× for 5 minutes versus 30 minutes) are from 2011 cross-industry research. No yoga-specific speed-to-lead study exists; the direction is robust, the exact multipliers are not yoga data.
  • There is no published benchmark for yoga-studio failed-payment rates, yoga-teacher churn, lead share by channel, or first-visit no-show rates for yoga trials. Where the report says "measure it," that is the honest answer.
  • The "80% of instructors leave every year" claim is unsourced and must not be used. The 20–40% involuntary-churn figure is SaaS data, not fitness data.
  • Contractor versus employee classification for instructors is a legal question, not a pricing choice. Get counsel before reclassifying anyone.
