Organizing Grok Bots as a Company Workforce

How a solo founder runs a team of Grok Bots like a company — without burning usage, and without letting a Bot drive your code editor by screenshots.

← Back to the podcast library · The corrected Grok Bots episode audio currently lives in the Library; this research companion is published here first.

Executive summary — the one-pager

  • What a Grok Bot is: a named, persistent AI teammate sharing one cloud computer per account (browser, filesystem, terminal), with memory and files between tasks, working while your laptop is closed. You create it by message, not by building a workflow. There is no model picker — that is a feature of the product, not a gap in this guide.
  • The company shape that works: one Chief of Staff as your single front door, specialists that each own one stable job, and handoffs through files, not through Bots chatting to each other. Group chats are the exception, for a live handoff or a disagreement you need to watch — not the default way work moves.
  • The usage truth: usage is metered by agent steps + tokens, not by messages. Every Bot reply, routine run, and bot-to-bot message costs, and every reply re-reads the whole conversation. Long chats, frequent routines, and chatty groups are the three leaks.
  • There is no per-Bot meter. Measure a Bot by testing it alone and watching the whole-account meter before and after. Do not claim a per-Bot usage split the app does not provide.
  • The coding trap: do not have a Bot drive Cursor by screenshots, and do not keep a Bot supervising a coding job in chat. The proper Bot route is a short, written spec handed to a Cursor Cloud Agent, which returns a branch/PR for you to review in Cursor. If you can already describe the change precisely, skip the Bot entirely.
  • Three pipelines, not one blob: researching ideas, running marketing, and building apps have different evidence and different gates. Do not let one Bot do all three, and do not let a research Bot's enthusiasm substitute for customer evidence.

1. What Grok Bots actually are

A Bot is not a chat. It is a persistent teammate with a job, memory, files, and a screen on a shared cloud computer. Boundaries live in chat instructions, skills, routines, and Auto Review rules — not in a settings field. Secrets live in the Vault, which is account-wide and cannot be limited to one Bot — do not try to separate secrets by Bot, because you cannot.

Skills and routines are the two repeatability tools. A routine is one Bot + a schedule or event, with limits reported as 50 per Bot, at least 5 minutes apart, and 300 runs per hour total / 200 per hour per routine. Design routines to end at drafts, because approval gates expire (see below).

2. Group chats — use sparingly, by design

  • Size: 2–6 Bots in a group. Mention (@) a single owner per stage; use @everyone sparingly.
  • Hard limits to design around: approvals, secret/login requests, and email/Slack drafts do not appear in groups; bot-to-group handoffs are text-only; voice chat is 1-to-1 only; every Bot reply counts toward usage.
  • Default: files for handoffs; groups only for a live handoff or a disagreement to watch. A group chat that becomes the default workflow becomes a usage leak and a quality leak at the same time.

Authority split, always: interpretation ≠ proposal ≠ execution ≠ spending. Grant alone decides spending, sending, publishing, merging, and granting access.

4. The pipeline every job follows

Intake → Triage → Work → Review → Approval → only then send / publish / pay / merge. Routines should be narrow and slow, one dedicated routine Bot per job, and should end at drafts. Unattended approval expiry is reported as about 10 minutes in docs-citing sources vs. 30 minutes in one Cursor help source — design for 10 minutes, so nothing important waits on an unattended approval.

Week 1, in order: (1) Chief of Staff, (2) Researcher, (3) Writer — with paste-ready first-message role descriptions in the full report, and first routines only after two clean manual runs. Weeks 2–4: one new Bot at a time, each only after three clean runs with no boundary miss. Coder only in week 4, when a repo and a real ticket exist.

5. Usage conservation — the only cost topic

How usage is metered: agent steps + tokens, in a shared weekly pool on the X Subscription (from Oct 12, 2026) covering Grok Bot + Cursor + Grok apps. Exact pool sizes are not published; third-party reports of tier multiples (Premium 4×, Super 15×, Ultra 50×) were not re-verified — check the current plan page before paying.

Fastest burns, in order: long chats (every reply re-reads the whole conversation); frequent or broad routines; chatty groups and bot-to-bot debate; vague, unbounded jobs; browser use where a connector exists.

Tactics that work: fresh chat per task; handoff summaries instead of endless threads; slow, narrow routines; one dedicated routine Bot per job; batching; capped scope; connectors before browser; pause or retire dead Bots; log the meter % before and after a job. To measure a Bot, isolate it: run it alone and compare the whole-account meter before and after.

What not to do in week one: do not launch five Bots at once, do not put every job in a group chat, do not set frequent routines before manual runs are clean, and do not let a Bot hold a job in chat while you wait on an approval that will expire.

6. Building apps: the Cursor trap, in full

RouteWhat it isWhen to use it
A. Bot drives Cursor by screenshotsWorst route: screenshot loop + chat re-read, no diff reviewNever as a working method
B. Bot supervises a Cursor Cloud AgentProper Bot route: returns a branch/PR, but costs Bot turns plus Cloud Agent turns ("a cloud agent is always Cursor usage")When a job starts outside the repo (customer report, research → spec, repro pack) or for async overnight staging
C. Cursor directlyCursor usage only, full diff/CI/merge reviewWhen the change is precisely describable, in an iterative diff loop, for auth/payments/data/infra/prod, or when short on usage

Avoid-trap procedure: spec file first → one ticket, one branch, one PR → Bot goes quiet after handoff → review the diff, not the story → merge only green, small, and confident.

7. Shared workspace discipline

  • One owner per file. The file is the record; a message is a nudge.
  • Memory and handoffs between Bots go through files and summaries, not through re-telling a long chat.
  • Secrets: Vault is account-wide. Never put a secret in a Bot-specific instruction expecting it to stay private to that Bot.
  • Errors: stop, record what happened, and hand back to the owner — do not let a Bot retry its way through a permissions or payment failure.
