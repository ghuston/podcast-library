How Health and Wellness Practices Make Money

For a consultant serving 3–15 clinician health and wellness practices: how they actually earn, where profit leaks, and how to sound like you know their economics better than they do.

← Back to the podcast library · Listen to the episode

Executive summary — the one-pager

The core idea: A practice does not sell hours. It sells attended, collected sessions. Profit lives in five gaps: inquiries that become first sessions, sessions actually attended, what each session collects after the payer's cut, what share the clinician keeps, and how many sessions the average client stays for.

  • Cash vs. insurance is not a simple choice. Cash-pay therapy typically runs $130–$185 a session; insurance averages $95–$125. Cash-pay practices reported about $15,000 more revenue but only about $3,000 more profit — marketing ate the difference.
  • Payer mix is pricing in disguise. For a 60-minute session, median reported rates are BCBS $123, Aetna $121, Optum/UHC $116, Cigna $95. That $28 top-to-bottom gap is about $32,000 a year at 25 sessions a week.
  • The funnel leaks at the top. Illustratively, of 100 inquiries, about 70 get a timely reply, about 45 book, about 35 attend, and about 28 are still there at session 4. Fixing the top compounds through every later stage.
  • Show rate and collection beat ads. In an 8-clinician worked example, lifting show rate from 85% to 90% is worth about $29,000 a year in contribution; lifting collection from 92% to 95% is worth about $30,600 a year, with no extra clinical hours.
  • Your real product is a join, not a dashboard. SimplePractice already reports income, no-shows, retention, and referral revenue. What it cannot show is profit per clinician or cost per acquired client, because it never sees rent, payroll, or marketing spend.
  • Retention is acquisition. At $115 collected, a 50% clinician split, and a $300 acquisition cost, payback is at about 5.2 sessions. A client who leaves at session 3 costs money; a 20-session client contributes about $850 after acquisition cost.

Figures are from the research report (Nov 5, 2026), with provenance and caveats below. They are US, 2025–2026 figures and vary by locality and payer — use them as a diagnostic model, not as a quote to a client.

The cost of a no-show: one empty therapy hour is $75–$200+. At $135 a session and a 20% no-show rate, a 20-client-a-week clinician loses about 4 sessions a week — about $540 a week, about $25,000 a year of gross capacity, before the clinician split.

3. The profit equation

Cost of Services (COS) target: 45–55% of revenue. COS is clinician pay + benefits + billing cost of delivering care + supervision/training. It excludes rent, admin, software, and marketing. If COS creeps to 65%, a $100,000 month leaves $35,000 for everything else.

Compensation modelTypical terms
1099 contractor, % splitClinician keeps about 60%, practice 40%; range 40–60% to clinician
W-2 employee, % splitAbout 50% is the common rule of thumb; range 45–65%
Hourly W-2$30–$50 per hour on the clock
Per-session flat rateExample: $40 per session

Profit target: 15–20% after owner compensation, per one advisory firm. Other guides quote 20–40%, but that usually means before a market owner salary — so always ask: "Is that profit before or after you pay yourself?" A practice that looks profitable only because the owner works unpaid clinical and admin hours is not a profitable business; it is a job with extra steps.

Worked example — 8 clinicians

8 clinicians × 24 booked slots/week, 85% show rate, $115 collected per session = about $81,305 a month gross collected; at a 50% split, about $40,653 contribution before overhead.

  • Show rate 85% → 90%: +$4,830/month gross, about +$2,415/month after split, about $29,000/year
  • Collection 92% → 95% on $85,000 allowed: +$2,550/month, about $30,600/year, almost all profit
  • One extra session per client, 40 new clients/month: +$4,600/month gross
  • One group a week (8 × $60) replacing one 1:1 hour: +$365/week for that hour, before group costs

4. Lead follow-up and nurture

Speed: reply within one business hour as the target, same day at worst. The famous "5 minutes vs 30 minutes = 21×/100×" multipliers are old, B2B-origin, and vendor-amplified — quote them as "an order of magnitude," not as fact. The defensible line is simpler: nobody in therapy shopping waits, and every hour after the first is a discount on your marketing spend.

A cadence that fits therapy: automated acknowledgement within minutes → human reply within one business hour, with one clear next step → second attempt on day 1 at a different time → value touch on day 3 (answer their question, confirm insurance) → check-in on day 7, with a waitlist offer if full → closing-the-loop touch on day 14, asking permission to keep them on the list → monthly nurture only with permission.

Good intake, in one checklist: one link, one short form (name, contact, preferred contact method, presenting concern in their words, insurance, availability, referral source); insurance verification before the first session, with expected out-of-pocket stated upfront; card on file and a published cancellation policy; reminders at 48 hours and 24 hours with a one-tap reschedule link; the same therapist from first contact through treatment (handoffs roughly double dropout in research summarised by Daryl Chow); and book the next session before the client leaves session one.

How to count the leak: for last month, how many inquiries arrived in each channel, and how many have a recorded reply within one business day? Any practice that cannot produce that count in 10 minutes has the leak, whatever its size.

5. Marketing, ranked by profit per retained client

  1. Referrals (therapist-to-therapist, physician, prescriber, school, community) — cash cost near $0, best fit, highest retention. Never pay for referrals. Its ceiling is relationships, not budget.
  2. Google Business Profile + local SEO + niche website — high intent, durable, owned by the practice. Prioritise for any practice with empty slots.
  3. Psychology Today + directories — $29.95/month, typically 5–15 inquiries a month, about $2–$6 per lead, but mixed quality and no built-in booking. Baseline, not a strategy.
  4. Insurance-panel listing / Zocdoc-style booking — $35–$110 per booked patient. Good for empty slots; bad as a default if already full, because then it just discounts the diary.
  5. Workshops, groups-as-marketing, school/employer partnerships — one event serves many prospects and positions the practice as the local specialist. Measure workshop-to-booking conversion.
  6. Content / SEO — compounds like local SEO, but slower; fund it from retained-client profit, not a launch budget.
  7. Paid ads, last — typically $200–$400+ per booked patient. Use surgically only, never to "get more leads" for a practice that cannot reply to the ones it has.

Acquisition cost vs. lifetime value: outpatient-therapy CAC is put at $150–$500. At $115 × 10 sessions = $1,150 gross lifetime value, a $300 CAC is a 3.8× gross ratio — but after a 50% clinician split the contribution lifetime value is only about $575, so the ratio on profit is about 1.9×, and at 4 sessions it is below 1×. That is why retention is an acquisition strategy.

6. Retention and lifetime value

  • Minimally adequate treatment = 8+ sessions; churn = leaving after fewer than 4 sessions. Always segment: % at 1 session, % at 2–3, % at 4–7, % at 8+. An average of 7 can hide a large cohort at 1 and a loyal cohort at 20+.
  • Groups: $40–$70 per person × 6–10 people = $240–$700 per clinical hour, vs. $110–$150 for 1:1. A single group of 8 at $60–$80 generates $480–$640 per 90-minute session.
  • Workshops: $60–$120 a seat for 2–3 hours, and also a lead generator.
  • Step-down and maintenance: biweekly/monthly maintenance, alumni groups, and check-in packages keep a client in revenue at low clinical intensity instead of discharging them to zero.
  • Managed waitlists: a stated expected wait, interim resources, group/workshop invitations, and same-week backfill of cancellations turn dead time into attended sessions. An unmanaged waitlist does the opposite.

7. The 10-question diagnostic + scorecard

Ask for last month's numbers, or last quarter's if monthly is too noisy. Accept "I don't know" — that answer is itself the diagnosis.

  1. Of every $100 you bill, how much do you actually collect — and how long does it take?
  2. What does each clinician collect per month, and what do you pay them, as a percentage?
  3. What is your show rate — attended ÷ scheduled — for first sessions versus ongoing sessions?
  4. How many inquiries did you get last month, from each source, and how many became a first attended session?
  5. How fast, on average, do you reply to a new inquiry — and who owns replies on weekends?
  6. What is your average number of sessions per client — and what percentage leave before session 4?
  7. What is your payer mix, and what does your lowest payer actually pay for a 60-minute session?
  8. How utilised is each clinician — attended sessions ÷ available slots — and who has open hours they cannot fill?
  9. What did you spend on marketing last month, and how many retained clients did it produce?
  10. After you pay clinicians, rent, admin, software, and billing — and after you pay yourself a market salary — what is left per month?

Scorecard greens (monthly, per practice and per clinician): utilisation 75–85%; show rate ≥90%; first-session show rate ≥80%; inquiry → first attended session ≥40%; first-response time <1 business hour; average sessions per client ≥8 with <20% churn; net collection ≥95%; initial denial <5%; days in A/R <40; clinician cost 45–55% of revenue.

Insider probes: Is that split on billed or collected? (Splits on billed pay clinicians for money the practice never receives.) Who chases a denial, and how old is your oldest unpaid claim? When a client cancels, does anyone work the waitlist that day?

8. What SimplePractice already does — and the real gaps

Already there (verified live from SimplePractice's own support docs, Nov 5, 2026): Practice Pulse on Plus (completed sessions, cancel/no-show rate, retention, income, average income per completed session, goals; daily refresh, fixed timeframes); Income reports; Income allocation by clinician on Plus, explicitly for payroll; Referral income (lifetime revenue by source, with a date-filter quirk — it populates by the date the client was added); claims, balances, and appointment reports.

Genuine gaps, verified by absence: no profitability after clinician compensation (no P&L in SimplePractice); no marketing spend vs. collected revenue, so no CAC or ROI by channel; no pre-client inquiry pipeline or response-time tracking; no single owner view across diary, billing, inquiries, marketing spend, payroll, and rent; no "revenue per clinician hour after split, by payer" view. Sell around SimplePractice (CRM + reporting join), not against it.

9. Boundaries: HIPAA, BAAs, and safe discovery

This is operational guidance, not legal advice. Any vendor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits protected health information on a covered entity's behalf is a business associate and must sign a Business Associate Agreement before PHI flows. Zoho offers a BAA template on request, and Zoho CRM is listed among services with HIPAA-supporting features — but "HIPAA-compliant CRM" is a configuration, not a purchase, and PHI must be confined to BAA-covered services only.

Discovery protocol: ask for aggregates, never exports — counts, rates, and dollar totals by source, payer, and channel. The CRM holds pipeline data (source, stage, dates, response times); it does not hold diagnoses, session notes, or treatment plans. Use hypothetical worked examples in the room, substituting the owner's own aggregate numbers live.

Caveats — say these plainly

  • No clean, published benchmark set exists for exactly 3–15 clinician practices; Heard's group figures come from a sample that is 86.5% solo. Per-clinician modelling is the honest substitute.
  • No-show and denial rates conflict by setting and definition (initial vs. final denials, different payer mixes). Always define the metric and measure the client's own rate.
  • Speed-to-lead multipliers and several marketing figures are vendor-sourced or old. Quote them with provenance, not as settled fact.
  • SimplePractice pricing and claim fees changed Oct 1, 2026 ($0.39/claim, free allowances removed, per third-party reporting) — re-verify on its current pricing page before quoting in a proposal.
  • All dollar figures are US, 2025–2026, and locality-dependent. This is research, not legal, tax, or clinical advice.
