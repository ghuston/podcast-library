One Pilates Studio, End to End

One small owner-operated reformer Pilates studio, eight beds, end to end: the bed-count revenue cap, the reformer trap, founding rates, occupancy, and the two leaks a consultant can sell — lead response at the top of the funnel and payment chasedown at the bottom. For a consultant learning these businesses well enough to serve their owners.

Executive summary — the one-pager

Read this in a couple of minutes. It is concepts only. Every figure lives in the detail sections below.

  • The bed count is the business. Capacity is reformers times classes per week, a hard physical cap. Nothing grows it except another bed or another class. Growth comes from fill and price, not from more hours.
  • The reformer trap. Reformer earns less per instructor-hour than mat, despite costing far more in equipment. It wins on price per member, premium positioning, and retention — not on throughput. Judge it by revenue per member, not revenue per hour.
  • Founding rates are forever. A discount locked in for life is a permanent haircut on the studio's best customers. It can be a good trade for pre-opening cash, but only as a capped, deliberate decision with a no-break clause.
  • Leak one: leads die in silence. Prospects message several studios in one evening and buy from the one that replies first. In Pilates the waitlist is part of the lead system, because a prospect who cannot get a peak-time bed assumes the studio is always full.
  • Leak two: sold is not collected. A failed autopay converted to a negative balance shows as successful on the autopay report. Arrears only surface in a different report, and members keep attending while owing money. Most of the money that ever comes back comes back in the first ten days.
  • The instructor is the retention strategy, and the category is saturating. Members follow teachers, not rooms, and reformer-certified teachers are scarce. The GLP-1 tailwind is real but unquantified, while same-store sales at the biggest franchisor have already turned negative on the coasts. A studio that fills its beds, answers its leads, collects what it is owed, and keeps its teachers does not need the category to keep booming.

Figures are from the research report (Nov 7, 2026), with provenance and caveats below. They are US, 2024–2026 figures and vary by locality — use them as a diagnostic model, not as a quote to a client.

1. The model studio: 8 beds, 30 classes, one room

The model studio in this report: 8 reformers, 1,200–1,800 sq ft, one room, 30 group classes a week, plus a small private and duet book. That is the size the research converges on for an independent start. A lean six-reformer studio in a second-generation space is the smallest viable version; a premium build pushes past a quarter of a million dollars.

Capacity is beds times classes. Eight beds times thirty classes is two hundred and forty sellable slots a week. At three quarters full that is about a hundred and eighty attended slots, and at roughly thirty dollars realised per attended slot that is about five thousand four hundred dollars a week of class revenue, roughly twenty-three thousand a month. Privates and duets add a further ten to fifteen percent in a healthy studio. Instructor pay at fifty dollars a class across thirty classes is about fifteen hundred dollars a week, roughly twenty-eight percent of that class revenue, inside the thirty-five percent payroll cap. The point of the model is not the exact total. It is that every lever in the business is visible in one line: beds, classes, fill, realised price, minus instructor pay, rent, and the equipment finance line. A consultant's first job in the room is to get the owner's real numbers for those five inputs and replace the model.

Startup cost is a wide range, and the range itself is the finding. Gymdesk puts a typical independent at $50,000–$250,000+ all-in; SmartHealthClubs $60,000–$200,000, with a lean six-bed studio at $60,000–$100,000; StartupModelHub $155,000–$437,000 for an 8–12 reformer leased studio with adequate working capital, and $90,000–$170,000 for a lean six-reformer second-generation site. The honest planning statement: a lean independent 6–8 bed studio opens for roughly $60,000–$170,000; a premium build pushes past $250,000. The single most consistent warning across sources is that owners budget the reformers and forget the working capital. StartupModelHub puts the cash needed to fund payroll, rent, and pre-sale marketing through the membership ramp at $60,000–$100,000, and SmartHealthClubs puts break-even at 12–18 months. The FTC's March 2026 settlement with Xponential explicitly disclaimed the franchisor's prior representation of six-month breakeven.

2. The price ladder — and the revenue-per-member number you must not quote alone

Revenue per member per month is genuinely disputed. LaunchAdvisor says $110–$160; MMCG says $200–$260 at maturity; Club Pilates' franchise disclosure implies about $188 (average gross $987,810 divided by 438 members divided by 12). The MMCG and Club Pilates figures are the stronger evidence because they are franchise-disclosure based; LaunchAdvisor's lower range likely reflects smaller independents and class-format counting. Use $150–$260 as the planning range and flag the source to the client. A consultant who quotes $110 will underprice a studio; one who quotes $260 will overpromise one.

The ladder itself, US 2026: reformer group drop-in $25–$50 (New York $50–$75); 4-class pack $89; 8-class pack $229; 10-class pack $300–$450; 8-class monthly membership $199–$249; unlimited monthly $149–$199 (LaunchAdvisor) to $199–$359 (MMCG premium coastal); private one-to-one $80–$150 a session; duo $65–$95 a person. Live studio price pages checked in November 2026 show the same shape at the premium end: Integr8 Pilates Charleston, founding unlimited $279 against regular $329; SĀMA Miami, founding reformer $270 against $320; Pilates BubbleBarr Tampa, early-member unlimited $199 against standard $369, 8-class $169, drop-in $25, intro three classes $59; IM=X Brooklyn, one-month unlimited trial $179, 12-month unlimited $270 a month.

Target revenue mix for a stable reformer studio: 60% recurring memberships, 30% packs and drop-ins, 10% retail, workshops, and events (Zatrovo, vendor guide). Club Pilates' disclosure reports memberships at an average 87% of gross revenue, which is expected for a franchise that sells almost nothing but memberships.

Founding rates are a permanent haircut, not a launch tactic. The standard pattern, verified across live price pages: 10–20% below the standard rate, locked in for life while the membership stays active, with a 2–3 month minimum. Common guardrails are a no-break clause (a card decline unresolved within five days, or any pause or cancel, permanently forfeits founding status) and a cap on the number of spots (SĀMA: 100; Soaked Studio: 30). The maths a consultant should do out loud: a hundred founding members at fifty dollars off is five thousand dollars a month, sixty thousand a year, for as long as those members stay, in exchange for pre-opening cash and a full room on day one. The safe design is to cap the count, tier the discount, and put a time limit or a no-break clause in writing.

3. The reformer trap — why the expensive machine earns less per hour

Mat: one instructor, fifteen to twenty clients, about $25–$30 a drop-in, roughly $450–$600 of revenue per instructor-hour. Reformer: eight beds, about six attending at three-quarters fill, about $30–$40 realised per slot, roughly $180–$240 of revenue per instructor-hour. Reformer revenue per hour trails mat despite 10–15× the equipment cost. The trap is judging the format by throughput. Reformer wins on price per member ($150–$260 against about $120 for a mat tier), premium positioning, and retention. A studio that adds mat classes to fill the schedule is adding a cheaper product that can cannibalise the premium one if the price gap is under about forty percent. The right use of mat in a reformer studio is as an entry product with a clear upgrade path, not as a parallel offer at a similar price.

Equipment economics: commercial reformers $3,000–$6,000 each (some premium models $7,000–$8,000); eight reformers outfitted $32,000–$48,000 before freight and tax; floor space 50–70 sq ft per reformer including room to move around it, so eight beds need about 840 sq ft of class space; financing $40,000 over five years runs roughly $800 a month (labelled arithmetic at about 8%); maintenance is springs, straps, and upholstery, and no clean published percentage of equipment value exists. One equipment manufacturer notes that build-out, not equipment, is the monster in a $200,000 build — treat that as a seller's framing, but the direction is corroborated by StartupModelHub, where build-out ($35,000–$120,000) and working capital ($45,000–$120,000) both exceed the reformer purchase.

4. Occupancy — the week, class by class

Benchmarks (vendor, converging): occupancy 75–85% optimal; below 70% leaving money; above 90% churn and burnout risk. Start 10–14 classes a week; add only when average attendance is above 65–70%. Dayparts run roughly morning 83%, evening 79%, midday 58% (Pepperoni, weak source — directional only). Occupancy is attended seats divided by available seats, per class and per daypart, never as one blended weekly average. Bookings are not attendance: a class can be 95% booked and 75% attended after no-shows.

No-show and late-cancel figures: 8–15% of booked seats (vendor guide); 10–20% cancellation and no-show combined (DojoBusiness, vendor guide). Pepperoni's published "benchmarks" of under 3% no-show and under 5% late-cancel are aspirational vendor targets, not observed medians — flag them as such. The 12-hour cancellation window is the near-universal standard, and the reason is the waitlist refill curve. In one worked example from four weeks of a six-reformer studio's bookings, waitlist refill rates were 86% for cancellations with more than 24 hours' notice, 64% at 12–24 hours, 33% at 4–12 hours, 17% under four hours, and 0% for no-shows. At an average yield of $25 a bed, 26 beds left empty over four weeks cost $650. The penalty design follows the payment type: pack holders lose one class credit; unlimited members pay a flat fee, typically $15 for a late cancel and $20–$25 for a no-show. The policy's purpose is refilling the bed, not collecting fees — waive the fee when the spot is refilled.

The intro-offer cliff: the drop-off after a three-class intro is a named retention killer; the fix is day-3 follow-up plus a time-limited conversion offer. A typical studio sees 5–10 new trial sessions a week and should aim to convert within 72 hours with automated follow-ups. No clean published Pilates-specific trial-to-member conversion benchmark was found; the boutique range of 20–45% applies, with paid intros converting far better than free (see §5).

5. Leak one: lead response, the cheapest fix in the building

Prospects message several studios in one evening and buy from the one that replies first with a warm answer and a named next step. About a third of fitness inquiries arrive after hours, when the front desk is teaching, not watching the inbox. The fix is an automatic acknowledgement in minutes, a human reply within one business hour, and a five-touch sequence through the trial. The speed-to-lead multipliers (21× for five minutes versus thirty; 23% of companies never respond) are 2011 cross-industry research — direction only, not Pilates data. No Pilates-specific speed-to-lead study exists.

Intro offers in the wild (live price pages, November 2026): BubbleBarr Tampa, three classes $59 within fourteen days; IM=X Brooklyn, $15 intro class or a $179 one-month unlimited trial; SĀMA Miami, single reformer class $49. Conversion has no Pilates-specific audited benchmark; the boutique range is 20–45%, with paid intros converting roughly 30–50% against free classes at 10–20% (Mujgos, verified live in the yoga research).

The Pilates-specific twist: the front door is narrower than yoga's. A reformer class has six to ten beds, so a prospect who cannot get a peak-time slot during their trial assumes the studio is always full — and no-show rates of 8–15% mean classes that look fully booked are not. The waitlist is therefore part of the lead-response system, not just an operations feature: a prospect who joins a waitlist and gets in is a conversion; a prospect who sees "full" three times is a lost lead. The consultant's diagnostic: "Show me last week's inquiries, how many got a first class booked, and how many of those showed — and how many waitlist offers went out?" One more structural gap: independent operator visibility in AI-enabled search (ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity) runs below 2% of recommendation responses per Metricus audit data cited by MMCG, which favours franchised brands. For an independent, Google Business Profile and reviews are the free compounding channel.

6. Leak two: collections, and what the software hides

The Mindbody arrears trap transfers directly — Pilates studios run the same booking software as yoga studios. A declined autopay converted to a negative balance shows as "Successful" on the Autopay Detail report; failed ACH and direct-debit payments never appear there at all. Arrears only surface in the Account Balances report (negative-only filter, aged 0–29 / 30–59 / 60–89 / 90+ days). Members keep attending while owing money, which is how a $200-a-month arrears becomes a $1,200 six-month arrears.

Failed-payment rate: no published Pilates-specific benchmark. The vendor range from the yoga research is 3–8% of monthly charges failing on first attempt, with some sources to 7–15%. The honest statement is "measure it from the client's own Autopay and Account Balances reports." Labelled arithmetic for a 150-member studio at $200 a month: 5% first-attempt failure is about eight failed charges a month, about $1,600 at risk; the recovery window is three to seven days, and most of the money that ever comes back comes back in the first ten days.

The consultant diagnostics: "Show me your Account Balances report filtered to negative-only, aged 90+ days — and what does Autopay Detail show for the same month?" and "What does the gap between what Autopay Detail shows and what hit the bank look like?" Green: negative balances under 1% of monthly dues; every failed charge touched within 10 days. Red: a 90+ day bucket the owner has never opened. Software gaps are the same as yoga: no P&L, no profit per teacher, no CAC, no lead-source-to-revenue in any of the booking platforms. Processing costs: Mindbody about 2.75–2.9% per transaction; Mindbody marketplace commission 20% capped at $30 on new clients booking through the Mindbody app.

7. Teachers: the retention strategy, and the pay model that changes the P&L

Reformer group class pay is $35–$60 a class (experienced instructors $60–$80+ in premium markets); mat group $30–$50; private one-to-one $80–$150 a session; duo $65–$95 a person; W-2 hourly $25–$50, with a salaried lead instructor at $55,000–$85,000 a year in major metros. Reformer-trained instructors command a 20–40% premium over mat-only. Indeed reported average Pilates instructor pay of $40.93 an hour in the US as of August 2026.

The payroll cap is 35% of gross revenue (Zatrovo rule, vendor guide). All-in instructor cost runs typically 20–30% above the nominal rate once payroll taxes, cover, and admin are counted. The model in §1 lands at about 28% on class revenue, which is inside the cap — but only because the schedule is full. At 60% occupancy the same 30-class schedule costs the same $1,500 a week against about $4,300 of class revenue, which is 35%. Payroll overruns in a Pilates studio are usually an occupancy problem wearing a pay problem's clothes.

Certification and scarcity: comprehensive certification costs $3,700–$6,000 and takes 450–500 hours (BASI, Polestar, STOTT, Peak, NPCP-recognised). Reformer certification moved from optional add-on to baseline hiring requirement in 2026. The MMCG outlook flags a documented sector-wide certified-instructor shortage as a named underwriting risk for lenders, and recommends named-instructor pipeline confirmation before financing a new unit. Members follow teachers, not rooms, and the teacher bench is the studio's scarcest asset. The diagnostic questions are who the members would follow if she left, and what the cover bench looks like. No state licence exists for Pilates instructors; certification is private. Scope risk: do not claim rehabilitation or treatment of medical conditions — the "rehab-adjacent" positioning that sells Pilates is also its claims risk.

8. ClassPass: filler, not funnel

Pilates was the most-booked workout on ClassPass for the third straight year in 2025, with overall reservations up 66% year over year and reformer bookings up 71% (ClassPass 2025 Look Back, via MMCG, verified live). That is precisely why the trap matters: the channel that brings the most demand pays the least per seat.

ClassPass does not publish what it pays. Operator-reported figures: $10–$15 a spot (718 Pilates, Brooklyn, in a public Instagram post announcing they would pull classes off ClassPass in 2026: "ClassPass pays only $10–$15 per spot, free trials result in zero payment to the studio"); Singapore operator interviews put payouts at S$10–$18 against S$22–$50 direct prices. The mechanism: ClassPass agrees a confidential rate floor with the studio, based on a percentage of direct pricing, then adjusts credits in real time (SmartRate); it says it "often pays a lower per-spot rate than a direct booking" because it only sells spots that would otherwise go empty. Payouts are monthly, roughly six weeks after the class. Free trials pay the studio nothing.

Conversion: ClassPass's own help centre says "ClassPass isn't designed to drive direct client conversions" and "ClassPass is NOT a lead generator; however, ClassPass typically does not decrease your direct client count" — illustrated as 100 direct clients before joining becoming about 102 after. Its users are price-sensitive and variety-seeking; 94% are new to the studio they visit, and 80% of those who become paying members return to a studio they tried through ClassPass, not with a direct membership. Third-party estimates put direct conversion at 3–6%. One studio owner reports ClassPass and Wellhub bringing in about $3,500 a month, 20–30% of revenue — at that share the studio is renting its peak seats to a channel that pays a third of the direct price and converts almost nobody. The caps: ClassPass at about 15% of revenue, 20% of bookings, and 25–30% of any class, and only in off-peak seats a member would not have taken. The consultant's diagnostic: effective rate per visit (total marketplace payout divided by marketplace visits) against the drop-in price.

9. The GLP-1 tailwind — real, unquantified, and not a forecast input

What is solid: a 2025 review estimated that 25–40% of total weight loss on GLP-1 therapy is lean mass rather than fat (cited by MMCG, secondhand — the review itself was not opened). Trial data from SURMOUNT-1 (tirzepatide) and STEP-1 (semaglutide) confirm lean-mass loss is a clinically significant feature. The standard-of-care response now includes resistance training, and reformer Pilates has emerged as the joint-friendly resistance modality of choice for prescribers and direct-primary-care providers serving GLP-1 populations (MMCG, verified live). About 12% of US adults have used GLP-1 drugs (Glofox, secondhand). Demand signals: reformer bookings on ClassPass up 71% in 2025; about 11.86 million Americans practiced Pilates in 2023, up 15% year over year; boutique Pilates studios were about 27% of all new boutique fitness openings in 2024.

What is not solid: MMCG states plainly that "quantitative attribution of new-member growth to GLP-1 referrals has not been published by any major operator." The evidence is anecdotal and channel-level. Do not put a GLP-1 number in a forecast. The demographic core is women 25–55 with household incomes above $65,000, about 72% of members nationally; men are 18–22% of new members in 2024–25 vintages, up from under 10% in 2018. The Gen Z "Pink Pilates Princess" TikTok phenomenon is driving trial among younger demographics and carries trend-cycle risk. Big gyms are entering: Crunch launched reformer Pilates across 20 locations in June 2026; Life Time is rolling a Pilates-inspired reformer program to 60+ locations. The tailwind lifts the category and invites better-capitalised competition at the same time. The consultant's use: GLP-1 is a positioning story, not a demand forecast. A studio can credibly market strength preservation, partner with prescribers and physical therapists, and program for the 45+ joint-friendly cohort. It cannot credibly claim "GLP-1 will bring us X members."

10. Saturation — the wave the tailwind is running into

Xponential Fitness systemwide same-store sales: 0.5% in 2025, down from 7% in 2024 and 16% in 2023; Q4 2025 turned negative at −4.3%, with Club Pilates at −3% (MMCG, verified live, from Xponential's Q4 2025 earnings release). Club Pilates ended 2025 with about 1,414 global studios and $1.14bn in system-wide sales; the US reformer studio count is projected to grow from about 1,800 at year-end 2025 to about 2,600 by 2030 (7.6% CAGR) — the growth is new units, not same-store growth. Saturated coastal cohorts: Los Angeles County 40 Club Pilates locations, Orange County 30, Maricopa County 26, Cook County 23, San Diego County 23; trade-area rings of 1.5–2.0 miles now overlap routinely in LA submarkets, and average unit volume there runs $850,000–$950,000 against a $1.3m top-quartile benchmark. Whitespace cohorts: Utah, Delaware, Colorado lead per-capita; Illinois, Georgia, Pennsylvania are the largest underpenetrated states by absolute population; secondary Sun Belt and Midwest metros (Salt Lake City, Boise, Madison, Des Moines, Greenville SC, Birmingham, Tulsa) post Year-3 mature average unit volume at or above $1.0m with rent-to-revenue 9–13% against 15–20% saturated coastal.

Club Pilates FDD 2026, Item 19 (verified live via FDD summary): 1,005 Qualified Studios averaged $987,810 gross revenue (median $978,332, range $146,258–$2,301,954), 438 average monthly active members, 6.4% monthly attrition (median 6.3%, range 3.4–13.7%), about 28 members a month to replace. Implied average life by arithmetic: 1 ÷ 6.4% ≈ 15.6 months. Memberships were 87% of gross revenue. Initial investment $413,289–$1,029,811 for a 12-reformer, 1,500–1,800 sq ft studio. The FDD discloses revenue only — no profit, EBITDA, or owner compensation. MMCG estimates mature-system EBITDA at about $197k on $984k revenue (about 20%), an estimate, not a franchisor disclosure. Regulatory overhang: the FTC secured a $17 million settlement against Xponential in March 2026, the largest consumer-redress amount in any Franchise Rule case, for misrepresenting buildout costs, time-to-open, and the former CEO's litigation history; a separate $22.75m class-action settlement was paid to 509 franchisees. For an independent, the relevance is reputational: the franchise next door may be under pressure, and its franchisees may be distressed sellers.

11. The 10 diagnostic questions — ask verbatim, of any Pilates studio owner

  1. Show me last month: how many inquiries, how many booked a first class, how many showed — and how many waitlist offers went out?
  2. What is your monthly churn, members lost ÷ members at the start of the month, and what is 1 ÷ that number?
  3. What did you actually collect per visit last month, collections ÷ visits attended, and how does that compare with your list price?
  4. What % of revenue went to instructor pay last month, including cover, taxes, and bonuses — and are you inside 35%?
  5. How many sellable beds did you have last week, and how many were attended, by class and by daypart?
  6. What % of revenue is recurring (memberships paid in advance) vs pay-as-you-go, and what happens to cash if no one new joins for 60 days?
  7. Who is your best client, how often do they come, what do they spend, and where did they come from?
  8. What is your cost to acquire one paying client, by channel, and what is that client's lifetime value?
  9. Which instructor, if they left tomorrow, would take clients with them, and what would that cost you?
  10. Show me your Account Balances report filtered to negative-only, aged 90+ days — and what does Autopay Detail show for the same month?

Numbers to use in a meeting

Use only these without further verification. Each is either a verified-live vendor benchmark, a franchise disclosure, or arithmetic from stated inputs.

  1. Club Pilates averaged $987,810 gross revenue and 438 members per studio in 2025, with 6.4% monthly attrition. Source: Club Pilates FDD 2026, Item 19, via FDD summary. Use it as the only audited-scale anchor in the room — and to say what it is not: a 12-reformer franchise, not a 6–10 bed independent.
  2. One point of monthly churn on 200 members at $200 a month is about $400 a month. Source: arithmetic from stated inputs. Use it to keep the churn conversation honest — and to show you check the math. The wide report's $2,200 figure for the same arithmetic at $110 was wrong by 10×.
  3. Reformer bookings on ClassPass grew 71% in 2025, and Pilates was the platform's most-booked workout for the third straight year. Source: ClassPass 2025 Look Back, via MMCG, verified live. Use it to show the demand is real — then the payout figure below to show why the channel still loses money per seat.
  4. ClassPass pays roughly $10–$15 a spot against a $35–$50 drop-in, and says itself it is "NOT a lead generator." Source: operator reports (718 Pilates, Brooklyn) and ClassPass's own help centre. Use it to make the case for capping the channel at empty off-peak seats.
  5. 25–40% of GLP-1 weight loss is lean mass, not fat. Source: 2025 review, cited by MMCG. Use it to sell strength-preservation positioning and prescriber partnerships — never as a forecast of how many members the tailwind will bring, because no operator has published that attribution.
  6. Xponential systemwide same-store sales were 0.5% in 2025, down from 7% in 2024 and 16% in 2023, with Club Pilates at −3%. Source: Xponential Q4 2025 earnings, via MMCG, verified live. Caveats — say these plainly

  • There is no audited small-independent Pilates P&L in the public record. The Club Pilates FDD is the only audited-scale dataset and it covers large franchised studios with 12 reformers and about 438 members — not a 6–10 bed independent. Every independent figure here is a vendor guide, a franchise summary, or a live price page.
  • Revenue per member per month is disputed: $110–$160 versus $200–$260 versus about $188 implied. Do not quote one number.
  • Startup-cost ranges span $60,000 to $450,000+ depending on format, city tier, and whether working capital is counted. Always get vendor and landlord quotes.
  • The GLP-1 tailwind has no published attribution to new-member growth. The 25–40% lean-mass figure is a 2025 review cited secondhand.
  • ClassPass payout figures are operator-reported and confidential by contract; $10–$15 a spot is the best available range, not a published rate card.
  • The "94% fill" reformer-versus-mat claim, the Pepperoni no-show and late-cancel "benchmarks," and the AI-reminder no-show reduction claims are vendor data.
  • The 35% payroll cap, the 75–85% occupancy band, and the 60/30/10 revenue mix are practitioner and vendor rules, not audited benchmarks.
  • Contractor versus employee classification for instructors is a legal question, not a pricing choice. Get counsel before reclassifying anyone.
  • Figures are US, 2024–2026, and vary by locality. Use them as a diagnostic model, not as a quote to a client.
