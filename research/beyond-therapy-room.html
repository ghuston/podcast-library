Beyond the Therapy Room: How Trainers, Yoga, Pilates, and Recovery Studios Make Money

Yesterday was therapy practices. Today is the rest of the health and wellness industry, wide first: personal training, yoga, Pilates, red light and recovery studios, massage, chiropractic, and IV and med spas for contrast. For a consultant learning these businesses well enough to serve their owners.

Executive summary — the one-pager

The core idea: Almost none of these businesses sell a visit. They sell a recurring relationship wrapped around a perishable slot: a class seat, a reformer, a treatment room, a sauna suite, or a trainer hour that expires whether or not it is filled. Profit lives in three things, in this order: practitioner pay, rent, and utilisation.

  • The five gaps transfer from therapy. Inquiries that become first visits, visits actually attended, what each visit actually collects, what share the practitioner keeps, and how long the average client stays. They bite in different places per type, but the diagnostic lens is the same.
  • Personal training is the retention weak spot. A one-to-one client typically pays $150–$500 a month, but the average client lasts only 3–6 months, and fewer than 15% reach 12 months. The escape is revenue per hour: $80 one-to-one, $160 semi-private (4 × $40), $200 small group (8 × $25).
  • Yoga and Pilates are class-capacity businesses. Yoga runs about $85–$115 per member a month, and teacher training is the profit engine. Pilates revenue per member is genuinely disputed: $110–$160 (LaunchAdvisor) vs $200–$260 at maturity (MMCG) vs about $188 implied by Club Pilates' franchise disclosure. Do not quote a single Pilates number.
  • Recovery is labour-light but not passive. One staff member can run a 2,500–3,000 sq ft floor, but utilities, cleaning, and equipment financing are the real costs. Paid intro offers convert 50–60%, free trials under 20%. Red light clearance is narrow: only certain acne, hair-growth, and pain devices are FDA-cleared.
  • Two leaks to check first. ClassPass pays $7–$14 per reservation versus a $22–$38 drop-in, and only 3–6% convert to direct membership. And churn compounds: cutting monthly churn by one point on 200 members at $110 a month keeps about $220 a month, not $2,200 (the $2,200 figure was wrong arithmetic, corrected here).
  • Chiro is the only insurance-shaped type, and narrowly. Medicare covers exactly one service: manual manipulation of the spine. A clinic lives on patient-visit average, visits per care plan, not new-patient count.

Figures are from the research report (Nov 5, 2026), with provenance and caveats below. They are US, 2024–2026 figures and vary by locality — use them as a diagnostic model, not as a quote to a client.

1. The one big idea: perishable slots

Almost none of these businesses sell a visit. They sell a recurring relationship wrapped around a perishable slot. Memberships and class packs exist to pre-sell that perishable inventory and to smooth cash flow, not primarily as a discount. Group formats (yoga, mat Pilates, bootcamp) leverage one practitioner across many payers; one-to-one formats (personal training, massage, chiropractic adjustment, private Pilates) cap revenue at practitioner-hours and live or die on price per hour and visits per client.

Revenue per client per month rises steeply with equipment and medical intensity: yoga about $85–$115, Pilates $110–$260 (range conflicts, see below), recovery $99–$199, massage $70–$120 on membership but $100+ per visit pay-as-you-go, personal training $150–$500, chiropractic about $100–$260 (2–4 visits a month at $50–$65), IV and med spa $185–$504 per visit, not per month.

The churn correction

A one-point monthly churn reduction on 200 members at $110 a month is worth about $220 a month: 2 members × $110. An earlier summary said about $2,200 a month. That was wrong arithmetic, and it is corrected here and in the episode. The point stands, just smaller: retention compounds every month, and it costs nothing in extra clinical hours.

3. Personal training — one-to-one, semi-private, group

Pricing: one-to-one $50–$150 an hour, typically $60–$70; semi-private $40–$60 per person; small group $20–$40 per person. Packs of 4 sessions $260–$480 and 8 sessions $480–$840 (LaunchAdvisor). Monthly retainers $150–$500 a month in person.

Revenue model: packages paid upfront dominate one-to-one historically — they eliminate no-shows and pull cash forward — but guides converge on monthly subscription for a set weekly frequency as the better model for group and semi-private: predictable revenue, higher retention, no per-session re-selling.

Unit economics: revenue per hour is the reason owners move up the ladder: one-to-one at $80 is $80 an hour; semi-private 4 × $40 is $160 an hour; small group 8 × $25 is $200 an hour (Gymkee). Ceiling: $80 × 25 sessions a week × 50 weeks is $100,000 a year at full capacity with zero time off. A realistic full schedule is 20–32 sessions a week.

Funnel: retention is the weak point: average client 3–6 months (TrueCoach; DojoBusiness, both vendor guides); under 15% last 12 months or 50+ sessions; average lifetime value cited about $1,075. No audited trial-conversion or first-visit no-show benchmark for personal training specifically. Boutique-fitness cost to acquire a client $45–$120 (IHRSA via vendor); personal-training-specific target under $100–$200 a client, replacement cost $200–$600 (vendor guides, conflicting with the IHRSA range).

Biggest leaks: empty trainer-hours (especially midday), no-shows on pay-per-session, discounting to fill (clients acquired at $25 a class feel cheated at $45), churn at month 3–4 (the modal exit point), and the owner being the capacity.

Regulation: no US state licenses personal trainers. NCCA-accredited certification (NASM, ACE, NSCA) is the de facto requirement of gyms and insurers, plus CPR and AED. Scope limit: cannot diagnose or treat injury, cannot prescribe individualised nutrition, cannot do physical therapy.

4. Yoga studios

Pricing: unlimited $99–$149 a month; 3× a week $79–$99; drop-in $22–$32; 10-pack $180–$250; 20-pack $320–$450; annual unlimited $900–$1,400 (LaunchAdvisor, mid-to-large US markets). Private $80–$120 an hour, up to $80–$200 in Tier-1 markets.

Revenue model: membership + class packs + drop-in, with teacher training and workshops as the high-margin engine: 200-hour training $2,500–$4,500 per student, 10 students is $30,000–$45,000 a cohort at low incremental cost. A studio selling only drop-ins "works ragged for a 5% margin" (Mujgos). Revenue per member $85–$115 a month (LaunchAdvisor).

Unit economics: 12 students × $16 effective (blended membership and drop-in) is $192 gross; instructor $50; about $60 an hour rent, utilities, and software; about $80 cleared before owner pay (Mujgos, illustrative). Instructor pay 25–35% of class revenue for group classes, so gross margin after practitioner pay is 65–75%, before rent.

Capacity: room seats × classes a week. Typical single-room studio: 1,200–2,500 sq ft, 25–30 classes a week. No audited occupancy benchmark for yoga specifically; the boutique-fitness rule of thumb is 70% attended-seat utilisation as the floor, 75–82% strong, under 60% closure trajectory (PulseRevOps, vendor guide). Full schedule is attended seats divided by available seats, not bookings: no-shows of 15–20% mask real utilisation.

Funnel: trial conversion 20–45% (boutique range, conflicting). Monthly churn 3–4% for premium and unlimited tiers (IHRSA via vendor); annual churn 30–40%. Average member life about 14 months at $120 a month is the illustrative example used across guides (lifetime value about $1,680) — treat as an example, not a benchmark. ClassPass pays $7–$14 per reservation versus $22–$38 drop-in, and converts only 3–6% to direct membership. Cap ClassPass at 15% of revenue, 20% of bookings, and 25–30% of any class.

Biggest leaks: empty off-peak classes, ClassPass dilution in seats a full-price member would have taken, discounting and intro-offer escalation in saturated markets, teacher-training dependence (lumpy, cohort risk), and the free-substitute problem: YouTube and apps at $19.99 a month cap what a live class can charge.

Regulation: no state licence for yoga teachers. Yoga Alliance 200-hour is a private registry, not a licence. No insurance reimbursement. Claims risk is general wellness and FTC substantiation: do not claim to treat disease.

5. Pilates studios — mat and reformer

Pricing: mat drop-in about $25, mat tier about $120 a month. Reformer group $25–$50 a session; 8-class membership $199–$249 a month; unlimited $149–$199 (LaunchAdvisor) to $199–$359 (MMCG premium coastal). Private reformer one-to-one $80–$150 a session; duo $65–$95 per person.

The revenue-per-member conflict: LaunchAdvisor $110–$160 a member a month vs MMCG $200–$260 a month at maturity vs Club Pilates implied arithmetic $987,810 ÷ 438 members ÷ 12, about $188 a month (2026 franchise disclosure). The MMCG and Club Pilates figures are the stronger evidence (franchise-disclosure-based); LaunchAdvisor's lower range likely reflects smaller independents and class-format counting. Use $150–$260 as the planning range and flag the source to the client.

Revenue model: target split for a stable reformer studio: 60% recurring memberships, 30% packs and drop-ins, 10% retail, workshops, and events (Zatrovo, vendor guide). Below 40% membership is too dependent on one-off sales; above 80% leaves acquisition revenue on the table.

The reformer trap: mat scales elastically: 1 instructor, 15–20 clients, $450–$600 an hour at $30 drop-in. Reformer is capped by machine count: 8 reformers × $30 × 6 attending is $180 an hour. Reformer revenue per hour trails mat despite 10–15× the equipment cost (PilatesBusiness, citing Mariana Tek 2026 Trends). Instructor: flat $35–$65 per group reformer class, or 25–35% of class revenue (group), 40–50% (private). Payroll cap: 35% of gross revenue (Zatrovo rule). Gross margin after practitioner pay: 65–75% on group reformer at typical fill.

Capacity: reformers × classes a week, a hard physical cap. Occupancy 75–85% optimal; under 70% is leaving money; over 90% is churn and burnout risk (vendor benchmarks, converging). Start 10–14 classes a week; add only when average attendance is over 65–70%. Equipment utilisation 60–75% for reformers.

Funnel: no-show and late-cancel without a policy loses 15–22% of potential class revenue (Zatrovo, 128 studios, vendor guide). Minimum: 12-hour cancellation window, credit forfeit, $15–$25 no-show fee at some studios. Churn target under 3% a month; over 5% a month is a retention problem (Zatrovo). Club Pilates franchise disclosure: 6.4% a month attrition, 438 average active members, about 28 members a month to replace — implying about 15.6 months average life by arithmetic, which reflects a large franchised base, not a small independent. Same-store sales: Club Pilates minus 3% in 2025; saturation in coastal markets is the 2026 story (MMCG).

Biggest leaks: over-scheduling (25–35 classes at 30% fill earns less than 14 at 70% and costs more instructor pay), underpricing reformer versus mat (under a 40% premium lets clients migrate to mat), no-show tolerance, founding-rate lock-in, and signing too much space (start 4–6 beds in 1,200–1,800 sq ft).

Fixed costs: reformers $3,000–$6,000 each; 12-reformer Club Pilates build $385,000–$839,000 all-in (2025 franchise disclosure) or $413,000–$1,030,000 (2026 summary — conflicting summaries, check the disclosure year). Rent-to-revenue 9–13% in whitespace markets versus 15–20% saturated coastal (MMCG). No clean published figure for maintenance as a percentage of revenue.

Regulation: no state licence for Pilates instructors; certification is private (BASI, Polestar, STOTT, Peak). No insurance. Do not claim rehabilitation or treatment of medical conditions. The "rehab-adjacent" positioning that sells Pilates is also its claims risk.

6. Red light therapy and recovery studios

Pricing: drop-in $29.50–$59 for a sauna and plunge suite (Sauna House Dallas); red light add-on $39 for 25 minutes, compression $30 for 30 minutes. Memberships: 2× a month $79; 4× $129; 8× $149; unlimited $199 a month (Sauna House); multi-modality tiers $149–$399. Infrared-sauna-only core tier $100–$160 a month (Boutique Benchmarks 2026, vendor guide).

Revenue model: membership volume, low labour. The model is "renting time in well-maintained rooms" (Honestly Profitable). Mature target mix by year 2: 65–80% membership, 15–25% add-ons, 10–20% drop-in. Illustrative (LIT, explicitly not a projection): 200 members × $199 is $39,800 a month; 400 is $79,600; 600 is $119,400.

Unit economics: one staff member can run a 2,500–3,000 sq ft open-community floor (LIT). Gross margin after practitioner pay is therefore very high (85–95% before rent and utilities) — but that flatters the model: utilities (chillers and heaters are the dominant operating cost), cleaning and sanitation between every session, towels and laundry, and equipment financing are the real costs. Stabilised single-studio owner net: $6,000–$25,000 a month (Honestly Profitable); year 1 often $0–$5,000 a month late in year 1, frequently break-even or loss while membership builds. No audited gross-margin percentage published — do not quote one.

Capacity: suites and rooms × hours; open-community capped at about 600 members per full build (LIT, vendor illustrative). Bottleneck: cold plunges — plan about 1 plunge per 5 sauna users, or members queue after heat and the experience (and retention) breaks.

Funnel: paid intro offers convert 50–60%, free trials under 20% (Boutique Benchmarks, vendor guide — the strongest conversion differential in this whole research; treat as vendor data, but the direction is corroborated by LaunchAdvisor's under-30% warning for free and cheap intros). Churn: top quartile under 2–3% a month; industry average 3.5–5%; healthy 2–3.5%; over 5% signals an onboarding or value problem (conflicting with the IHRSA 3–4% boutique range; use 2–5% as the range). Three-tier structures lift retention 15–20% versus single-tier.

Biggest leaks: permanently underpricing founding rates ($99 locked forever), launching without pre-sales (empty room on day one), under-provisioning cold plunges, underestimating utility load, treating it as passive too early, and over-promising health benefits — disappointed members churn and complain.

Fixed costs: single private suite (sauna + plunge bundle) from $12,000; full open-community build $100,000–$250,000 (LIT, equipment vendor — verify against independent quotes). Infrared cabins $4,000–$18,000 each; total startup $40,000–$250,000 (Honestly Profitable). Cold plunge: commercial tub $3,000–$15,000+, chiller $2,350–$13,000. Bagged ice is the trap: about $17 per use, $1,500–$4,500 a month, versus $15–$55 a month for a chiller. Red light panels and beds: $2,000–$40,000 as an add-on line.

Regulation — the claims trap: no licence to operate a recovery studio in most jurisdictions, but that is not the same as unregulated: zoning, building permits for water and electrical, water fill, drain, and filtration (some jurisdictions regulate plunges like commercial pools), electrical capacity, ventilation and moisture, ADA, liability insurance and waivers, and safety screening (pregnancy, cardiovascular conditions, photosensitivity, implants). Red light claims are the sharpest regulatory edge in this report. FDA 510(k)-cleared indications are narrow: certain acne, hair-growth, and musculoskeletal-pain devices only. Everything else (skin health, recovery, sleep, energy, longevity) is off-label and must be marketed under the FDA General Wellness Policy — "may support", not "treats"; "cleared", not "approved"; registration is not clearance. FTC enforcement zones: body contouring and fat loss, weight loss, anti-ageing. Warning letters: Spectra Therapy (July 2025), Dr Joel Kaplan (January 2024).

7. Massage therapy practices

Pricing: national average $102–$112 an hour (Vagaro 2026 benchmarks via Avvale); Swedish and wellness $70–$100 an hour; deep tissue and sports $95–$130 an hour (20–30% premium); 90 minutes $90–$175. Membership: $70–$120 a month for 1 session a month (rollover), pioneered by Massage Envy at $70–$100. Zenoti massage tiers $59–$89, $99–$129, $149–$189.

Revenue model: hybrid pay-per-visit + membership. Members visit 10.4× a year versus 2.1× for non-members (Massage Envy figure via FasterCapital, secondary) and at 1.3–1.5× the rate of non-members (Zenoti). Membership-focused spas: median ticket $144 versus $103 non-membership (Zenoti 2026 Benchmark, 2025 data).

Unit economics: therapist pay: commission 35–45% of session revenue ($33–$43 on a $95 session); W-2 $18–$28 an hour base, loaded $21–$33 an hour. Gross margin after practitioner pay: 55–65% on commission; about 70–80% on W-2 loaded cost at $102–$112 pricing — but W-2 carries payroll tax, workers' comp, and idle-time cost when the book is not full. The owner-therapist's margin is 100% before imputing their own wage (BLS median $57,950 a year).

Capacity: rooms × therapist-hours. A therapist's sustainable hands-on load is about 18–25 sessions a week; above about 25–30 hands-on hours a week burnout and turnover rise (practitioner consensus, no clean published benchmark). Full schedule is booked room-hours divided by available room-hours, adjusted for no-shows.

Funnel: repeat rate typically 60–85% (Orbitpeek, weak source); spa repeat-client retention 75%, average 4.88 visits a year, top performers 7–8 (Meevo via FaveCard, vendor guide, method unclear). No audited trial-conversion, first-visit no-show, or average-lifetime-months benchmark found for small massage practices. Membership retention target: at least 85% monthly member retention (Zenoti, vendor guide).

Biggest leaks: empty room-hours and last-minute gaps (no waitlist discipline), therapist turnover (clients follow the therapist, not the studio — the single biggest structural risk), discounting (Groupon-style acquisition trains price-sensitive clients), no-show and late-cancel without a card-on-file policy, and membership breakage handled badly.

Regulation — licensed, but not medical: state licence or certification in most states (for example Pennsylvania: at least 600 hours approved instruction + MBLEx national exam; California: CAMTC certification, AB 1504 effective January 1, 2026 recognising massage as a health-care service). Scope: soft-tissue manipulation only. Excludes diagnosis or treatment of impairment, illness, disease, or disability, and excludes chiropractic adjustment and physical therapy. Insurance: Medicare does not cover massage therapy ("You pay all costs"). Private coverage is exceptional (workers' comp or auto with a prescription, HSA or FSA with a doctor's letter). Do not build a model on insurance reimbursement.

8. Chiropractic clinics

Pricing: initial exam and consultation $60–$200; standard adjustment $50–$90 a visit (cash); cash membership $99–$199 a month for a set number of visits. Insurance reimbursement $40–$80 a visit on average; about $42 a visit after adjustments, write-offs, and collections (BPA, vendor guide); Medicare 98941 national average about $38.41 in 2026. Cash niche programmes (neuropathy, knee, disc decompression): $2,800–$4,800 per patient per programme (BPA — vendor claim, high-ticket and high-scrutiny).

Revenue model: the clinic lives on patient-visit average (PVA) — visits per care plan — × average visit value, not new-patient count. A 12-visit plan is worth about 3× a 4-visit plan. Mix: insurance (volume, lower per-visit, delayed and denied collections), cash memberships and packages (paid at time of service, higher per-visit), personal injury (higher per-visit, slow and uncertain payment), and ancillary (rehab, massage, decompression, retail and supplements).

Unit economics: solo median collections $300,000–$500,000 a year; multi-provider, rehab, and ancillary clinics $750,000–$1.5 million+ (Crestmont). Planning model: 150 visits a week × $55 is about $400,000 a year (150 × $55 × 48 weeks is $396,000; assumes about 48 working weeks). Net margin 20–35% after all expenses but before doctor compensation above a market salary (Crestmont) vs 15–30% (Avvale) — conflicting; use 15–35% and note the definition (before or after owner comp) drives the spread. Cost structure (Crestmont, industry-survey based): payroll and benefits 20–30% of collections, occupancy 8–15%, malpractice $2,000–$10,000 a year, marketing 5–10%, EHR $300–$800 a month.

Capacity: visits a day — adjustments are 10–15 minutes, so throughput, not rooms, is the constraint. A solo DC at 25 visits a day is a full book; 150 a week is the planning model. Full schedule is visits divided by available visit slots, plus patient-visit average (are patients completing plans?) and reactivation (are discharged patients returning for maintenance?). The leak is not empty rooms; it is plan drop-off (patient stops at visit 4 of 12) and maintenance patients drifting away.

Funnel: new-patient channel: word-of-mouth and referral is the single largest (Crestmont), ahead of paid ads and physician referral. Retention is measured as patient-visit average and as maintenance conversion, not monthly churn — there is no published monthly-churn benchmark for chiropractic comparable to fitness. Frequency example: a suburban clinic's average fell from 2.8 to 2.1 visits a month across 400 active patients, 280 fewer visits a month, about $14,000 lost at $50 a visit (Spinesly, single-clinic anecdote — a sensitivity illustration, not a benchmark). First-visit no-show: no clean benchmark found.

Biggest leaks: plan non-completion (the patient-visit-average leak), insurance write-offs, denials, and slow collections (the $42-collected vs $50–$90-charged gap), maintenance patients lost to "wellness" competitors, discounting packages, and single-payer or single-referral-source concentration (over a third of collections from one source is volatility, Crestmont).

Regulation — the only insurance-shaped type in scope: licensed in all 50 states: Doctor of Chiropractic degree + NBCE exams + state licence; scope varies by state. Medicare covers exactly one service: manual manipulation of the spine to correct a diagnosed subluxation (CPT 98940–98942), 80% after the Part B deductible; X-rays ordered by the DC, exams, modalities, extraspinal manipulation (98943), and maintenance care are not covered; AT modifier required to show active and corrective care. Chiropractors cannot opt out of Medicare. Commercial insurance: pre-authorisation, visit limits, and lower reimbursement for the same CPT codes are routine. Cash practice is a legitimate model, but the transition risk is real: income drops while cash patients fill the gap, and most practices that switch cold-turkey reverse within 90 days (BPA).

9. IV hydration and med spas — brief contrast only

Ticket: IV $100–$400 a session, average about $280; NAD+ $500–$1,200; blended ticket in one model $185. Med spa average ticket $504 a visit (AmSpa via Avvale); Botox $10–$25 a unit by region; average clinic revenue about $1.4 million a year (AmSpa 2023, 10,488 spas, 81% single-location).

Margin: IV gross quoted 55–85% — conflicting, because consumables are quoted at $10–$50 per drip (DripMap) vs about $75 per drip (Avvale); net 20–40% for a well-run clinic (DripMap). Med spa net 15–25% established (AmSpa via ProvenSuccess) vs 20–40% in templates — conflicting; use 15–30%.

Regulation: practice of medicine. Medical director + standing orders + Good Faith Exam (MD, NP, or PA, not RN) required in most states; corporate practice of medicine doctrine — in strict states (California) the clinical entity must be at least 51% physician-owned, non-clinician uses MSO and PC structure. Medical-director retainer $500–$2,000 a month (mobile IV) to $1,500–$8,000 a month (med spa, California and Texas top). No US state had IV-spa-specific legislation as of June 2024 (Yale and CSPI, JAMA Internal Medicine, October 2025); only 4 states had guidance on all four oversight categories; 85%+ of clinics recommended cocktails for symptoms without taking a history; 1 in 4 required a prior medical consult; 0.8% cited evidence for benefit claims. In California only MD or DO or RN and PA under delegation may inject — LVN, MA, and esthetician categorically barred; paying a director a percentage of revenue risks illegal fee-splitting — fixed fee instead.

Contrast in one line: IV and med spa has 3–10× the ticket of a yoga or Pilates visit, but the owner cannot simply own the revenue — the clinical entity, the prescriber, and the exam are legal prerequisites, and the fixed medical-director + compliance cost is owed at 40% utilisation and at 95%.

No clean first-visit no-show benchmark exists for any type — the consultant must measure it from the booking system in week one (inquiries → booked → showed). Speed-to-contact, paid (not free) intro, and a named next step at booking are the levers. Enforce a 12-hour window + credit forfeit; automated waitlists fill about 81% of cancelled spots vs about 34% manual (vendor claim, directional). Measure attended seats, not bookings. Track realised dollars per visit (collections ÷ visits; attended revenue ÷ attended seats), not list price. Cap the aggregator at 15% of revenue / 20% of bookings. The diagnostic question is always: "What is 1 ÷ monthly churn?" — that is the implied lifetime, and most owners have never done the division.

11. The 10 diagnostic questions — ask verbatim, of any of these owners

  1. "Show me last month: how many inquiries, how many booked a first visit, how many showed?" — Gives gaps 1 and 2 in one answer. If they cannot produce the three numbers, the booking system (not marketing) is the first job.
  2. "What is your monthly churn — members/patients lost ÷ members at the start of the month — and what is 1 ÷ that number?" — Implied lifetime in months. Most owners quote annual retention; monthly churn is the operable number.
  3. "What did you actually collect per visit last month — collections ÷ visits attended — and how does that compare with your list price?" — Gap 3. Exposes ClassPass dilution, insurance write-offs, and discounting in one ratio.
  4. "What % of revenue went to practitioner/instructor pay last month, including cover, taxes, and bonuses?" — Gap 4. The 35% Pilates payroll cap and the 40–55% personal training split are the reference points.
  5. "How many sellable slots did you have last week, and how many were attended — by daypart?" — Utilisation by morning, midday, and evening. A blended 75% can hide a 58% midday and a 95% peak.
  6. "What % of revenue is recurring (memberships/packages paid in advance) vs pay-as-you-go, and what happens to cash if no one new joins for 60 days?" — The survival question. Below 40% recurring (Pilates benchmark) is fragile; recovery and massage should be higher.
  7. "Who is your best client — how often do they come, what do they spend, and where did they come from?" — Tests whether acquisition spend matches the actual buyer (and whether referrals are tracked at all).
  8. "What is your cost to acquire one paying client, by channel — and what is that client's lifetime value?" — Lifetime value to cost ratio. If cost to acquire is unknown, marketing budget is a guess. Healthy rule of thumb: at least 3:1.
  9. "Which practitioner, if they left tomorrow, would take clients with them — and what would that cost you?" — The key-person and therapist-follows risk (massage, personal training, chiropractic associate, star Pilates and yoga teacher). No non-compete or rebooking system is unpriced risk.
  10. "What can you legally claim, and who is allowed to do what here — licence, scope, prescriber, and the exact words on your website?" — Regulation. For recovery, IV, and med spa this is the first question, not the tenth.

13. What is genuinely different from therapy — and what transfers directly

Genuinely different: no insurance (except chiropractic, narrowly) — the client is the payer, so price, perceived value, and churn hit revenue immediately, with no payer buffer and no collections lag. Class-based capacity: therapy sells one-to-one hours; yoga, Pilates, and bootcamp sell seats in a room at a time, so one practitioner hour produces 6–20× revenue, and the binding constraint is fill rate. Equipment-heavy (Pilates, recovery): a therapy practice's capital cost is a room and a chair; a reformer studio's is $3,000–$6,000 per machine × 6–12, and a recovery studio's is $12,000 per suite to $100,000–$250,000 per build. Membership-heavy and retail-like (recovery, yoga, Pilates): recovery is substantially self-serve (one staff member for 600 members), and yoga and Pilates sell autopay memberships where revenue arrives whether or not the client attends. No licence and low barrier to entry (personal training, yoga, Pilates, recovery): anyone can open, certification is private, and the moat is retention, not licensure. Claims risk is marketing risk, not billing risk: red light, sauna, and IV claims are policed by FDA and FTC substantiation, not by a payer's medical-necessity review.

What transfers directly: the five gaps transfer almost intact, and the diagnostic questions are the same questions with different nouns. Patient-visit average is therapy's visits-per-episode; plan completion is the same problem as therapy's dropout after visit 4. Practitioner-share maths: the 35–45% massage commission, 40–55% personal training split, and 25–35% Pilates class share are the same negotiation as a therapist's split — and the same trap: if the practitioner keeps over 50% and the owner also pays rent, the owner has a job, not a business. Retention compounds the same way; the lifetime-value-to-cost ratio of at least 3:1 and the "replacement costs more than retention" logic are identical. No-show and late-cancel policy, waitlists, rebooking at checkout, and reactivation of lapsed clients are the same operating disciplines, with the same payoff, in every type. Owner-as-practitioner confusion: in personal training, massage, and solo chiropractic — as in solo therapy — the profit and loss flatters the business until the owner's wage is imputed.

Caveats — say these plainly

  • No audited, small-studio benchmark exists for first-visit no-show rate, cost per acquired client by type, or average client lifetime in months for most types. What exists is vendor-platform data, franchise disclosures (Club Pilates, YogaSix — large, franchised, not small independents), and practitioner guides. Treat every number as a planning range to be replaced by the client's own books in week one.
  • Pilates revenue per member is the sharpest conflict: $110–$160 (LaunchAdvisor) vs $200–$260 at maturity (MMCG) vs about $188 implied by Club Pilates' 2026 franchise disclosure. Do not quote a single number.
  • The churn correction: a 1-point monthly churn reduction on 200 members at $110 a month is about $220 a month, not $2,200. The $2,200 figure in the research report's summary was wrong arithmetic.
  • Churn figures except Club Pilates' 6.4% come from vendors with something to sell. The 75–85% Pilates occupancy figure is vendor-platform data; yoga has no equivalent published benchmark. Recovery-studio gross and net margins have no audited margin published; the $6,000–$25,000 a month stabilised owner-net figure is from a single operator guide.
  • Equipment maintenance as a percentage of revenue (reformers, plunges, saunas, red light beds) has no published benchmark; budget from vendor service contracts, not from industry data.
  • Med-spa and IV figures are franchisor and association summaries (AmSpa) and vendor guides, not audited small-independent financials; treat $1.4 million average revenue and the $504 ticket as association benchmarks, not as a promise for a single-location startup.
  • Conflicting counts: US chiropractic businesses about 40,000 (Crestmont and Census) vs about 70,000 (IBISWorld via Avvale); Club Pilates 2026 franchise-disclosure investment range differs between secondary summaries — always cite the disclosure year.
  • All dollar figures are US, 2024–2026, and locality-dependent. This is research, not legal, tax, or clinical advice.
