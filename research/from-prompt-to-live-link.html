1. Cursor Build Live Apps — describe it, get a link

What happened. On November 5, 2026, Cursor launched Build Live Apps. You start a cloud agent and tell it explicitly that you want to build an app. The agent scaffolds the web app, spins up a Postgres database if the app needs one, puts authentication behind Cursor's own sign-in (SSO), and provisions hosting. When you publish, you pick a unique domain and Cursor hands back a live link. It can deploy from new or existing repos on Cursor Origin and GitHub; GitLab support is described as "coming soon".

The gate matters more than the generation. Before anything goes live, Cursor runs deterministic and agentic security checks — the changelog and docs name checks for secrets written into app files, credential files, .env files, and source maps. Nothing goes live until the checks pass and you explicitly publish. Admins can see every app and secret and unpublish any app. Public sharing is off by default for Teams and Enterprise until an admin enables it; on individual plans you can share apps publicly.

Availability and price. Available on all paid plans with cloud agents enabled. Free and Plus do not include cloud agents — Plus is the $8/month plan — so in practice this starts at Premium, $30/month, and up. Cursor has not published what hosting costs beyond the plan, or whether there is a usage-based charge on top, as of November 6. No hostname pattern, quotas, backups, or data-export terms were published either. Treat those as open questions, not as included.

What it means for Grant. This closes the gap his own work keeps hitting: writing the code got cheap, getting it off localhost did not. The Guitar Tracker he has been building in Cursor — documenting his late uncle's 120–150 guitar collection for his aunt in Dana Point — could become a link she opens, no install, no localhost. On the consulting side, a client intake form, a lead tracker, or a small dashboard for a Pilates studio becomes a live link instead of a slide in a proposal. That is the done-for-you offer in its most literal form: a working tool, shipped as a link. Start with internal tools — a booking tracker, a client intake form, a team checklist — and do not put customer payment details into something built over lunch.

2. Claude Personal Finance — your bank accounts in Claude, read-only

What happened. On November 5, 2026, Anthropic started rolling out Personal Finance, a beta that lets you link bank, credit card, loan, and investment accounts to Claude, see balances and spending on one page, and ask questions about your money. It connects through Plaid, the same account-linking provider behind many finance apps. It is US only, and on the Pro, Max, and Ultra plans.

What it can and cannot do. It is read-only: it cannot move, manage, or invest money, and it carries no ads or sponsored products. Anthropic stresses read-only access and says it does not train on your financial data. It is described as being about your own accounts — personal, not business.

What it means for Grant. He already tracks spending in Monarch against a $3,500 monthly budget, and he is working through roughly $7,800 he owes Ana. Claude will not replace Monarch, and it should not be the system of record. Its honest use is the question he actually asks: which subscriptions crept up this month, how much of last month was business versus personal, where did the money go. One nudge that matters: if business and personal spending share one card, separate them first, or the answer will be mush — and the same separation is what his own bookkeeping needs anyway. Worth trying as a second opinion; not worth reorganizing his finances around.

3. Images 2.6 and Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash — GIFs and video by conversation

ChatGPT Images 2.6 (November 4, 2026). OpenAI's image generation update is live in ChatGPT across every subscription tier, on desktop, mobile, and web. Three changes: images generate about 50% faster, per OpenAI; you can animate an uploaded photo or a text prompt into a looping GIF, adjust playback speed, and edit individual frames; and edits are steadier, so characters, products, and brand details stay put across revisions instead of slowly melting. The API version also exports SVG files, which drop straight onto a website or into print.

Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash (generally available November 5, 2026). Google DeepMind's model makes video, with audio, from text, images, audio, or video — and, more importantly for a non-editor, edits video by conversation: you tell it what to change and it changes it, rather than you dragging clips around a timeline. Clips run 3–15 seconds in one-second steps (up from 10 seconds), at 720p for $0.07 per second, roughly 30% cheaper than Omni 1.1, with upscaling to 1080p or 4K. It is distributed through the Gemini app, YouTube, Google Flow, AI Studio, and Google Workspace, though no date is promised for any given account. Two guardrails: videos carry SynthID watermarks so AI content can be identified, and Google is restricting the ability to change what a person in a video says, for now.

What it means for Grant. Short-form content without hiring an editor. A guitar photo becomes a looping GIF for a listing. A 15-second clip explains a service for a studio owner. The same rule as always applies, and it is worth saying out loud: check every frame. AI still loves to quietly misspell a label, add an extra finger, or change a detail that matters to a customer. Never edit a customer, a client, or a member of staff to say something they did not say — not even as a joke.

4. The acceleration signal — METR's assessment of Anthropic

What happened. On November 5, 2026, METR, the nonprofit that evaluates AI systems, published "Independent Assessment of AI R&D Acceleration at Anthropic in October 2026", after an 11-week high-access evaluation from August 12 to October 29, 2026, with about 2.5 full-time-equivalent staff, internal benchmarks, training data about the research process, and roughly 15 staff interviews. METR took no payment; Anthropic provided laptops and free tokens.

The headline number. Anthropic is likely improving its models' capabilities on well-specified tasks at about twice the rate it did between March 2024 and October 2025. The central measure, AECI (Anthropic's version of the Epoch Capabilities Index, which turns benchmark scores into a single score), grew at 1.9× its earlier rate since October 2025, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.5× to 2.8×. METR reads that as roughly two years of historical progress in a single year — about the distance from Claude 2.1 to Sonnet 4.5. METR's subjective estimate is that a majority of the acceleration as of October 2026, perhaps about 80%, is due to Anthropic using AI for its own research. METR calls the 80% figure highly uncertain, and says it would not be surprised if the true acceleration were closer to 1.3× or 3× the historical rate.

The redactions are part of the story. Anthropic had the right to redact any non-public information, and METR says it "redacted a majority of the evidence we found most informative for these conclusions". Anthropic described the redactions as aimed at removing confidential information that could accelerate other AI developers. METR also reviewed the automated-R&D section of Anthropic's August 2026 Risk Report and disagreed with it in a way that matters: Anthropic's claim not to have crossed its "2× Acceleration Threshold" — an early warning in its Responsible Scaling Policy — rests on "contestable judgment calls", it is "plausible" the threshold had been crossed by July 15, 2026, and the public report "overall understates the level of acceleration that was occurring". METR also says it has not seen evidence that AI can yet make highly significant, fully autonomous contributions to the most important parts of model training.

What it means for Grant. The direction is the takeaway, not the decimal. The tools are improving faster because AI is doing more of the research, which is why a weekly catch-up keeps mattering: what was a demo last month is a shipped feature this month, and what is gated to defenders today is a product tomorrow. The practical response is the one the episode closes on — ship small, finished, useful things while everyone else argues about benchmarks. His edge was never picking the model; it is the workflow, the approval rules, and the client relationship around it, and those get more valuable as the models get faster, not less.

5. DeepMind, RSA-1024, and GPT-6 Cyber — the safety story, read to the second paragraph

What happened. On November 6, 2026, Google DeepMind announced that human-guided AI agents had uncovered two major weaknesses in legacy 1024-bit RSA, an encryption standard that is decades old. The work was led by DeepMind researchers Dj Dvijotham and Abhradeep Thakurta, with cryptographers Nadia Heninger (UC San Diego / Inria) and Emmanuel Thomé (Inria), and published as two working papers.

The two findings. First, a new way to forge RSA digital signatures where an attacker can influence part of the data being signed: DeepMind estimates it is about 1,600× more efficient than the previous best method for short 32-byte digests. In a sandboxed reproduction on an already-solved 1024-bit benchmark key, it used 3.5× less CPU time (391 CPU core-years against 1,380 for the original team). Second, a batch-factoring method ("fan factories") that, after a one-time precomputation equal to the cost of factoring a single 1024-bit number, factors each further 1024-bit key at about 1/58 of the cost of a standard run — roughly 30× cheaper than the best previous batch method. Both big numbers are theoretical estimates from math, not demonstrated breaks of live systems; no implementation of the batch method exists, the papers had not been peer-reviewed at announcement, and no independent group had replicated them yet.

What is not affected. Modern RSA keys of 2048 bits or larger are not threatened, and DeepMind and the papers say so plainly. The public factoring record is 896 bits. US federal guidance (NIST) has disallowed RSA keys shorter than 2048 bits for generating new digital signatures since 2013, though 1024-bit keys still survive in older hardware, long-lived certificates, and embedded systems. The longer view: NIST's draft transition plan would deprecate 2048-bit RSA after 2030 and disallow RSA entirely after 2035, because of quantum computers — a separate, slower-moving clock.

GPT-6 Cyber, same week. On November 5, 2026, OpenAI announced GPT-6 Cyber, a specialist model for authorized security researchers to identify software vulnerabilities, develop working exploits, and create fixes. OpenAI says it found 948 ways to exploit Linux. It is gated to authorized researchers, not in the general chatbot — the same capability that finds the flaw is the capability that could use it.

What it means for Grant. Nothing he uses today broke, and that is the honest headline. What broke is old crypto that should have been retired years ago, and the fix is boring hygiene: inventory old keys, retire 1024-bit RSA, move to 2048-bit or better. That is exactly the kind of unglamorous, checkable fix a consultant gets paid to find for a small business — and the DeepMind story is a good example to carry into a room, because it shows AI doing real, supervised, expert work (weeks, with cryptographers steering) rather than acting alone. The dual-use point cuts both ways and is worth naming to clients: the tool that finds the vulnerability is the tool that could exploit it, which is why access controls and approval rules are part of the product, not an afterthought.

6. Also this week — worth knowing, not worth a segment

  • Perplexity Teammates (Nov 4). AI agents that own an ongoing job instead of waiting for a prompt — Perplexity's examples include watching competitors for price changes, drafting replies to support emails using your policies, and checking your inbox each morning with drafts ready. Max subscribers only, web first, iOS to follow. The guardrail example in Perplexity's own material is the right instinct: "Check with me before any refund." If you are not already paying for Max, do not upgrade for this yet.
  • Perplexity Computer for ecommerce (Nov 3). From one brief, Computer can look at Shopify sales, recommend a collection to push, build the landing page, set up Meta ads, and write the Klaviyo emails to match — with a budget cap and review gates you set, including publishing pages, sending emails, and spending on ads. Only worth it with clean product data; an AI reading messy stock data will confidently promote the thing you have run out of.
  • Firecrawl in ChatGPT and Claude (Nov 2 and Nov 6). A people and company data tool inside ChatGPT or Claude — 250 million people, 26 million companies, work emails and company data, starting with 1,000 free credits a month — plus SEC filings from over 8,000 public companies, including annual reports and insider trades. A decent prospect list and a quick read on a big customer before a pitch, from a chat window. A work email from a database is not permission to email someone.
  • Gemini 4 Argon (Nov 6). Google is testing it with cybersecurity professionals through its Fairwind Program. It has a 1-million-token output limit, against 64,000 previously, and Google reports 77.9% on DeepSWE v1.1 and 91.7% on LVBench. A development of Gemini 4 Carbon (Oct 28), still gated, no general-access date.
  • Thinking Machines Lab, Inkling 2 Small (Nov 5). A mixture-of-experts model with about 594 billion total parameters and 17 billion active, supporting image inputs and variable effort, with Apache 2.0 weights planned. One to watch for the open-weights side of the ledger.
  • OpenAI BioBasicsBench (Nov 6). With enzyme-design company Ligo, a benchmark for how AI agents handle routine wet-lab science tasks. Narrow, but a sign of where agent evaluation is heading: real lab work, not chat.
  • Google's $17B Wyoming data center. Announced this week, relying partly on a planned 702-megawatt fuel-cell development. The infrastructure build-out behind all of the above keeps getting bigger.
