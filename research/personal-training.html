The Personal Training Business: Solo Trainers and Small Studios

A deep dive on one business type: solo trainers and small studios with three to fifteen people on staff. The ceiling is hours, the escape is leverage per hour, retention is the leak, and sold is not collected. For a consultant learning these businesses well enough to serve their owners.

Executive summary — the one-pager

Read this in a couple of minutes. It is concepts only. Every figure lives in the detail sections below.

  • The ceiling is hours, not demand. A trainer can only be in one room with one client at a time, and a diary fills up long before the business feels big. So the question is never just how to get more clients. It is what one hour is worth, and how many of those hours can actually be sold.
  • The escape is more payers per hour, not more hours. Semi-private and small-group coaching let one coach earn more in an hour while each client pays less than one-to-one. That only works if the group stays small enough to feel coached, and full enough to beat the one-to-one hour.
  • Retention is the leak. Clients leave in months, not years, and the decision is made in the first few weeks. A studio that does not measure who is still here by cohort is re-selling its whole book every few months without knowing it.
  • Sold is not delivered, and delivered is not collected. A package sale is a promise, not profit. Sessions that never happen and payments that fail quietly are routinely misread as churn, and the owner celebrates a big sales month while borrowing from the future diary.
  • The paper split is not the real split. What a trainer actually keeps of what was actually collected is almost always lower than the headline split, because of discounts, no-shows, unbilled sessions, and failed payments. Pay on delivered and collected, and count every paid hour, not just coaching hours.
  • The marketing that works is boring and local. Referrals first, a complete Google Business profile second, one sustained visibility channel third, paid ads last. And the cheapest fix in the business is replying fast, because the person who replies first usually wins the client.

Figures are from the research report (Nov 5, 2026), with provenance and caveats below. They are US, 2024–2026 figures and vary by locality — use them as a diagnostic model, not as a quote to a client.

1. The one big idea: the ceiling is hours

A personal training business sells time, and time is the one inventory that cannot be restocked. Demand clusters in the early morning and the evening, midday is soft, and programming, admin, marketing, and travel eat the hours around the sessions. A diary that looks full is often around twenty-five paid hours wrapped in a fifty-hour week, and most trainers hit a wall at about twenty-five to thirty sessions a week before quality drops.

That makes personal training a capacity business, not a volume business. Growth has only four routes: charge more per hour, put more payers in the hour, earn income that is not tied to an hour (online or hybrid coaching), or hire. Drifting to thirty-five sessions a week is not a strategy. It is burnout with a waiting list.

The owner being the capacity is also the fragility. Illness or a holiday means zero revenue from that trainer, and a solo trainer with no systems is a diary, not a business. That is the first thing to check in the room: is there a business here, or a very busy person?

2. Solo trainers: three ways to get paid

Most solo trainers work under one of three arrangements, and which one is best is a volume question, not a moral one.

ModelHow it worksWho bears the risk
Employed by a gymHourly or per-session pay. The gym keeps most of the session price; the trainer is often paid for only a fraction of it, and sometimes does unpaid floor hours.Gym bears rent, marketing, admin. Trainer bears low pay.
Revenue split / commissionTrainer keeps a share of the session fee, commonly around half, with the gym keeping the rest. Large chains can take up to about seventy percent.Shared. Trainer bears income volatility; gym bears facility cost. Key question: who owns the client if the trainer leaves?
Rent-a-floor (flat rent)Trainer keeps all session fees and pays flat rent, roughly $1,000–$2,500 a month in the guides, or an hourly floor rate.Trainer bears all fixed cost in slow months. Best once the book is full and predictable.

The crossover is computable. At a typical session price, a forty-percent gym split costs about thirty dollars a session, so flat rent of about $1,500 a month breaks even at roughly eleven sessions a week. Above that, rent wins. Below it, the split wins, before counting the leads the gym supplies, which a pure rent deal usually does not. The other solo-trainer fact that matters: a full diary can still be a low hourly wage once unpaid hours are counted. Around a third to almost half of a trainer's working week is unbillable.

Breakeven for a solo trainer is roughly eight to fifteen regular weekly clients in a city; reliably profitable is more like twenty to twenty-five active clients. Those are vendor-guide figures, not audited benchmarks, but they give the shape: the business is thin until the book is full, and full means a lot of clients, not a lot of hours.

3. Small studios (3–15 staff): leverage per coach-hour

A three-to-fifteen-person studio is typically an owner-operator plus a few coaches and part-time front desk or admin help. At the top end there is usually a lead coach or studio manager. Payroll is the largest cost line; rent is second. There is no published, audited dataset for US PT studios of exactly this size, so this section is built from franchise disclosures, consultancy benchmarks, and vendor guides, and the gaps are named in the caveats.

FormatClients per coachPrice per person/sessionRevenue per coach-hour
One-to-one1About $80–$150$80–$150
Semi-private2–3About $40–$60$80–$180
Small group4–6About $20–$40 (some guides $30–$60)$120–$360

The leverage, in one line: at four clients paying about forty dollars each, one coach-hour earns about a hundred and sixty dollars, double the one-to-one hour, while each client pays half the one-to-one price. The catches are ratio discipline (four to six clients is the sweet spot; below four you are doing discounted one-to-one; above eight you have become a class), scheduling complexity (assigned groups and time slots, not drop-in), and programming overhead (individualised programming inside a group framework takes more prep than a single class workout).

Fill rate is the whole game. A small-group slot at half fill earns no more than a one-to-one hour, with more complexity. The consultancy utilisation targets are ninety to one hundred percent for one-to-one and seventy to eighty percent for small group. Ask for revenue per available coach-hour, not per delivered hour, and split peak from off-peak, because demand clusters at six to nine in the morning and four to eight in the evening.

Trainer pay in studios is where the consultant earns their fee. Market splits of fifty to sixty percent to the trainer are common for contractors, but the consultancy house rule for employed trainers is twenty-five to thirty-five percent of the session price, with total people costs (excluding the owner) under forty percent of gross revenue. These are not a contradiction once you ask who bears what: a contractor on a fifty-percent split usually bears their own tax, insurance, certification, and programming time, while an employed trainer's studio bears employment costs, paid non-session time, and benefits. The diagnostic question is not "is fifty percent too high?" It is "who bears what, and do total people costs stay under forty percent once every paid hour is counted?"

Rent is the second cost line. Boutique studio space runs roughly $18–$45 per square foot per year, and a small PT space is typically 1,000–3,000 square feet. The rule of thumb is to keep base rent below fifteen to twenty percent of monthly revenue. A two-thousand-square-foot space at forty dollars a foot costs about $6,700 a month, which already breaches that rule on thirty thousand dollars a month of gross revenue. That is a one-line test to run in the room.

For scale, the closest franchise concept, Alloy Personal Training (semi-private), reported average unit revenue of about $394,000 a year across seventy-three units in its 2026 disclosure. That is franchisor-reported revenue, not profit, with survivor bias, and it is not what an independent studio makes. It is useful only as a sense of what a working semi-private model can gross.

4. The five gaps for PT

These are the diagnostic core. Each gap has a formula, and most owners do not measure most of them.

Gap 1 — Inquiry to first session

Define each stage separately, because most owners blur them: enquiries, contacted, consultation or trial booked, showed for the first session, paying client. The guide ranges are roughly seventy to eighty percent of enquiries contacted within a day, forty to fifty percent of contacted prospects visiting, and thirty to forty percent of trials converting to paid, with elite studios at fifty to sixty percent. The most common leak is no follow-up at all. A Harvard Business Review audit of over two thousand US companies found twenty-three percent never responded to a web lead at all, and the PT equivalent is the direct message or form that sits unread until the prospect has joined someone else. Green: at least half of enquiries get a consultation booked, at least seventy percent of booked first sessions show up, and the owner can state both numbers.

Gap 2 — Attendance and no-shows

No-show rates for gyms and classes run roughly ten to twenty percent in the vendor guides, with well-run studios under ten percent and anything over twenty percent a structural gap. No clean PT-specific benchmark was found. It matters more in PT than in classes because a missed one-to-one hour cannot be resold at short notice, while a missed seat in an eight-person group costs an eighth of the hour. That asymmetry is the commercial case for deposits or payment at booking, a clear late-cancel and no-show policy (commonly twenty-four hours, session forfeited), and automated reminders. The policy only works if it is enforced, so ask how many no-show fees were actually charged last month. Two missed eighty-dollar sessions a week, unpaid, is over eight thousand dollars a year.

Gap 3 — Collection per session

Three different numbers, and owners routinely use one word for all three. Sold is the value of packages and memberships sold in the month, cash in but a liability until delivered. Earned is the value of sessions actually delivered. Collected is cash actually received. Unearned revenue, sold minus delivered, is a liability, not profit. Failed-payment rates in the vendor pages citing IHRSA run from three to twelve percent a month, and the two attributions contradict each other, so do not quote either as an IHRSA finding; say the range itself tells you nobody has a clean number and the owner should measure their own. Green: collected divided by sold above ninety-five percent in the month, failed payments listed and chased within forty-eight hours, dunning automated, and trainer pay tied to delivered and collected sessions, not sold packages.

Gap 4 — Practitioner share

For each trainer, per month: sessions delivered, amount collected for those sessions, amount paid to the trainer, realised share (paid divided by collected), and revenue per trainer-hour collected. The paper split is not the realised share, for all the reasons in the collection gap. Greens are the consultancy house rules: total people costs under forty percent of gross, trainer at twenty-five to thirty-five percent of the session price. Reds: any single trainer carrying more than fifteen percent of total studio revenue, which is concentration risk, and trainers paid on sale rather than delivery, which rewards selling over retaining.

Gap 5 — Client lifetime and retention

The guides converge on an average PT client life of three to six months, with up to half of clients lost in a year. The often-quoted "fewer than fifteen percent reach twelve months" is stated in one vendor guide with no source, and it conflicts with the only measured dataset found: one online coaching platform, five thousand six hundred and sixty-six clients, where forty-five percent were still active at month twelve. Those are different populations, US in-person PT guides versus Nordic online coaching, so neither disproves the other, but neither number can be quoted as the PT retention rate. Worse, that platform's own summary is internally inconsistent: a forty-five-percent survival rate at twelve months implies a mean lifetime of at least five and a half months, which contradicts the four-point-three-month mean in the same summary. Cite the survival curve, not the mean, and label it online, Nordic, one platform.

For context, gym members are better measured: the Health and Fitness Association's 2025 benchmarking report gives sixty-six point four percent annual retention for gym members, about three point four percent a month. That is gym members, not PT clients, and PT is higher-touch and higher-price, so a reasonable working bar for PT is monthly churn under five percent, offered as judgement, not a benchmark. Churn maths worth doing slowly: monthly churn of eight percent means half the roster is gone within about nine months. One point of monthly churn on two hundred members at a hundred and ten dollars a month is about two hundred and twenty dollars a month, not two thousand two hundred. And do not annualise monthly churn by multiplying by twelve: three percent a month compounds to about thirty-one percent a year, not thirty-six.

5. Lead follow-up and marketing that actually works

Speed to lead is the strongest evidence in this section, and its limits matter. The twenty-one-times figure, a lead contacted within five minutes being about twenty-one times more likely to qualify than one contacted after thirty minutes, is real peer-reviewed business-to-business research on thousands of web leads. It is not PT-specific, and the episode says so. The direction transfers: the person who replies first wins. The practical fix is unglamorous: an automatic acknowledgement within minutes, a human reply within the hour during waking hours, and a seven-day structured follow-up sequence, not a single "just checking in."

Channels ranked by return, per the guides:

  1. Referrals and word of mouth. Near-zero money cost, highest intent. One survey cited second-hand says sixty-nine percent of trainers get most clients this way, but the methodology was not verified, so treat it as directional. Ask at the milestone moment, make bringing a friend free and normal, thank every referrer.
  2. Google Business Profile. Free, compounding, very high intent. A complete profile with real photos of coaching and steady reviews costs nothing but consistency.
  3. Being seen coaching. Short-form video of real coaching is the most-cited organic visibility lever in the 2026 guides. No audited conversion rate found.
  4. Local partnerships. Physios, salad bars, condo managers, corporate wellness. Cited across guides, no measured conversion rate found.
  5. Google Ads. Very high intent, works in one to four weeks, but only after the organic offer converts predictably.
  6. Meta and Instagram ads. Interruptive intent. Works for trial offers, but trial no-shows run high without a reminder system.
  7. Lead marketplaces. You pay when you reach out, not when the client books, so a dead lead still costs money. Judge strictly on cost per acquired client.

No audited, PT-specific cost-per-acquired-client figure was found. That is itself a finding. The healthy ratio repeated across guides is lifetime value at least three times acquisition cost, per channel, not blended. A blended acquisition cost hides the truth: a channel at one and a half to one that feels busy is losing money once time is costed. Compute it per channel: ad spend plus marketplace fees plus the value of staff time on that channel, divided by new paying clients from that channel in the same period. Lifetime value is average monthly revenue per client times average months retained, using collected revenue, not sold.

6. The 10 diagnostic questions — ask verbatim, of any PT owner

  1. Show me your last ninety days: inquiries, consultations booked, first sessions showed, paying clients, by source. What is the conversion at each step?
  2. What is your no-show rate on paid one-to-one sessions last month, and how many no-show fees did you actually charge?
  3. Last month, how much PT did you sell, how much did you deliver, and how much did you collect? What is sitting in unearned revenue?
  4. For each trainer, what did their sessions collect last month, and what were they paid? What is their realised share?
  5. What percentage of the clients who started twelve months ago are still here? And six months ago? By cohort, not overall.
  6. What is your revenue per available coach-hour, peak and off-peak separately, and your fill rate per slot?
  7. What are your total people costs as a percentage of gross revenue, including the owner, front desk, and payroll tax?
  8. What did your last ten new clients cost you to acquire, per channel, including your time?
  9. Who owns the client if a trainer leaves, and what does their contract actually say about non-solicitation, programming IP, and notice?
  10. What happens in the first fourteen days after a client signs: onboarding, check-ins, progress baseline, and who is responsible for it?

Bonus question for the owner-coach: how many hours a week do you coach, and how many hours do you spend on admin, marketing, and programming that nobody pays for?

8. Regulation and scope — what a consultant must get right

No US state requires a government-issued licence to practise as a personal trainer. Certification is the de facto ticket: most commercial gyms, insurers, and corporate wellness employers require an NCCA-accredited certification (NASM, ACE, NSCA, or ACSM) before hiring or covering liability, and CPR and AED are usually required too. A licence is government-issued and legally required to practise; a certification is issued by a professional body for meeting competency standards. Personal training has the second, not the first.

Scope limits are real and are a liability issue. In scope: exercise programming for apparently healthy or medically cleared clients, health-history screening, fitness assessment, behaviour-change and adherence coaching, and general nutrition education such as MyPlate, macronutrient concepts, hydration, and portion awareness. Out of scope: diagnosing any medical condition or injury, treating injury or disease or rehabilitation (that is physical therapy), prescribing individualised meal plans or medically driven diets, and clinical nutrition therapy, which is reserved to registered dietitians and physicians. A nutrition-coaching certificate extends general coaching; it does not confer dietetic scope, and the line varies by state, so the safe line in a meeting is "check your state's dietetics practice act." A named referral network, physical therapist, dietitian, physician, mental-health professional, is a core responsibility, not a last resort.

Business hygiene to check: liability insurance current, certification and CPR and AED expiry dates tracked, waiver and health-history process in place, independent-contractor versus employee classification for trainers, who owns client data and programming if a trainer leaves, and cancellation and no-show terms in writing.

Caveats — say these plainly

  • There is no audited, US-representative, PT-only profit-and-loss dataset in the public domain for 2024–2026. The Bureau of Labor Statistics is the only audited anchor. Almost everything else about PT studios is vendor guides, franchise disclosures, or single-platform data.
  • No audited dataset exists for US PT studios with three to fifteen staff specifically. Franchise disclosures for Alloy, F45, and StretchLab are the closest, and they are franchisor-reported revenue with survivor bias, not profit.
  • The "fewer than fifteen percent reach twelve months" claim is stated in one vendor guide with no source. The only measured dataset found disagrees. Quote neither as the industry rate; ask for the owner's cohort survival.
  • The Coachway platform summary is internally inconsistent: a forty-five-percent survival rate at twelve months implies a mean lifetime of at least five and a half months, contradicting the four-point-three-month mean in the same summary. Cite the survival curve, not the mean.
  • Failed-payment rates of three to six percent and seven to twelve percent a month are both vendor pages citing IHRSA, and they contradict each other. Neither was verified against the primary report.
  • The "five percent retention improves profit by twenty-five to ninety-five percent" claim traces to one bank's branch system, not fitness. Do not use it as a fitness fact.
  • "Eighty percent of personal trainers quit within two years" appears in vendor guides citing other vendor guides, with no primary study found. Do not use it.
  • Alloy disclosure figures were read via a secondary summary, not the disclosure document itself. Verify against the disclosure before quoting in a client meeting.
  • Do not present certification as a licence, do not suggest a trainer can prescribe a diet because they hold a nutrition certificate, and do not give state-specific legal advice.
